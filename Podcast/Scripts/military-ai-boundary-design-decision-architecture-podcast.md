This is the Decision Design Podcast from Insynergy, examining how judgment, authority, and accountability must be redesigned for the age of AI.

Who decides, and where is the line?

That is the central question behind the current standoff between the U.S. Department of Defense and Anthropic. And it matters because the dispute is widely described as an ethics conflict, when in fact it is something more basic and more difficult: a structural failure in how judgment authority is allocated between states, corporations, and autonomous systems. The issue is not simply what AI should do. It is who gets to decide what AI may do, under what conditions, and with what limits.

The public framing is convenient. It suggests a familiar contest between a safety-conscious AI company and a powerful military institution. But that frame misses the deeper design problem. What is unfolding is not primarily a values dispute. It is a design vacuum.

In July 2025, the Pentagon awarded contracts worth up to $200 million each to four frontier AI companies: Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, and xAI. The objective was to integrate advanced AI into national security operations. Anthropic’s Claude became the only model deployed on classified military networks, operating through a partnership with Palantir. The arrangement held, until it did not.

In January 2026, Claude was reportedly used in the operation to capture Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro. That disclosure triggered the confrontation. Anthropic maintains two non-negotiable constraints: no mass domestic surveillance of Americans, and no fully autonomous weapons without human oversight. The Pentagon sees those constraints as operationally unworkable. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth said publicly that the military would not use AI models that restrict warfighting capability. Senior officials threatened to designate Anthropic a “supply chain risk,” a classification normally reserved for foreign adversaries. At the same time, xAI accepted the Pentagon’s “all lawful purposes” standard across all classification levels, while OpenAI and Google signaled flexibility. Anthropic stood alone.

Around the same moment, Anthropic disclosed a second pressure on the system. Three Chinese AI laboratories — DeepSeek, Moonshot AI, and MiniMax — had conducted industrial-scale distillation campaigns against Claude, generating over 16 million exchanges through approximately 24,000 fraudulent accounts. Anthropic warned that the distilled models likely lack the safety guardrails built into the originals, creating national security risks of a different kind.

So Anthropic is under pressure from both sides. On one side, a domestic military wants safeguards removed. On the other, foreign adversaries extract capabilities while stripping those safeguards away. That is the structural situation. And it is why this cannot be understood as a morality play. It is an engineering problem, one that existing governance language does not adequately solve.

Anthropic is not opposed to military uses of AI in principle. Dario Amodei has written explicitly that democracies have a legitimate interest in some AI-powered military and geopolitical tools. The company was the first to deploy a frontier model on classified networks, and it has provided customized models for intelligence customers. What it refuses is more specific: mass surveillance of its own citizenry, and weapons systems that fire without human intervention. Those are boundary claims.

The Pentagon’s objection is also specific. Officials argue that the gray areas between surveillance and intelligence collection, between autonomous targeting and automated support, are too numerous and too context-dependent for a private company to adjudicate case by case. As a senior defense official put it, it is unworkable for the military to negotiate individual use cases with a vendor. That position is also rational. Both sides are internally coherent. Neither is obviously unreasonable. And that is precisely the problem.

This is not a conflict between right and wrong. It is a conflict between two legitimate claims to decision authority over the same domain, with no shared mechanism for resolution.

That is why four familiar frameworks fall short.

Governance is useful when principles have already been agreed. But here, the principles themselves are contested. “All lawful purposes” and “human-supervised limited use” are not just different implementations; they are competing axioms.

Digital transformation is also relevant, but it asks how to digitize. It does not ask what should remain outside the scope of digitization. The Pentagon’s GenAI.mil initiative, described by Undersecretary of Defense Emil Michael as central to building an “AI-first” enterprise, is a textbook transformation effort. Yet the real question is not how to adopt AI. It is where to draw the line around adoption.

Automation improves speed by removing bottlenecks. But the meta-judgment — what to automate and what to reserve for human decision — cannot itself be automated without losing judgment altogether. The Pentagon’s frustration with case-by-case review reflects that limit.

And AI ethics, while important, does not solve the problem either. Multiple ethical frameworks can coexist. Anthropic’s standard differs from U.S. law, which differs from international humanitarian law. The question is not which ethics are correct. The question is who has authority to apply which standard in which context.

That brings us to the central claim: this is a boundary allocation problem.

Look at the symmetry. The Pentagon demands the removal of safeguards in order to maximize operational capability. Chinese laboratories bypass safeguards entirely through illicit extraction, deploying stripped-down models without constraint. One pressure comes from within democratic governance; the other from outside it. But both reveal the same structural deficit: there is no agreed architecture for allocating judgment authority between states, AI providers, and the systems themselves.

Anthropic’s red lines are corporate policy decisions. The Pentagon’s “all lawful purposes” standard rests on sovereign legal authority. China’s distillation campaigns ignore the question of authority altogether. All three actors are acting rationally within their own frames. The system stalls because no shared frame exists.

That is why the useful concept here is Decision Design. Decision Design is the practice of explicitly structuring who holds judgment authority, over what scope, and under what conditions. It designs decision authority, decision scope, and decision conditions. In other words, it does not determine what the right decision is. It determines where the right decision gets made. It creates the architecture within which accountable decisions can be produced.

“Decision Design is a judgment architecture framework proposed by Ryoji Morii, founder of Insynergy Inc., for structuring authority, accountability, and decision boundaries in AI-augmented organizations.”

In the Pentagon–Anthropic dispute, the question is not simply whether autonomous weapons are ethical. The question is whether authority to determine permissible use resides in a vendor’s terms of service, a sovereign legal framework, or a jointly governed body, and what happens when those authorities conflict.

Decision Design is not governance, because governance operationalizes principles that have already been agreed. It is not ethics, because ethics asks what should be valued. It is not compliance, because compliance checks adherence to existing rules. Decision Design asks who writes the rules, where their jurisdiction ends, and when authority shifts.

The practical value becomes clearer in implementation. A Tri-Layer Decision Model can separate AI use-case evaluation into three distinct layers.

At Layer 1, legality belongs to national legal institutions: military legal counsel, judge advocate general, domestic law, international humanitarian law, and rules of engagement. The question here is whether a proposed use case is legally permissible. The AI provider has no role in making that legal determination, because legality is a sovereign function.

At Layer 2, capability safety belongs to the AI provider’s technical safety team. The criteria are model reliability, hallucination risk, output fidelity, and failure modes. The question is whether the model can perform the proposed function safely and predictably. The state’s role is independent third-party verification of safety claims.

At Layer 3, strategic appropriateness belongs to a joint body of defense officials, AI provider representatives, and independent evaluators. The criteria are long-term strategic impact, alliance compatibility, escalation risk, and precedent effects. This is where Anthropic’s concerns about mass surveillance and autonomous weapons structurally belong — not as blanket ethical objections, but as strategic-risk assessments requiring shared deliberation.

The practical benefit of separating these layers is that it makes disagreement legible. It does not eliminate disagreement. But it stops the system from collapsing legality, technical safety, and strategic judgment into a single contractual phrase. That collapse is where most confusion arises.

A second operational tool is the Decision Ledger. Traditional audit logs record what happened. A Decision Ledger records who decided, on what basis, within what scope, and whether escalation occurred. It would capture decision type, AI involvement scope, human intervention point, applicable authority layer, and escalation flag.

The value is straightforward. First, it improves post hoc accountability. In cases like the Maduro operation, a structured record of Claude’s involvement would replace ambiguity with evidence. Second, it enables adaptive boundary calibration. As operational data accumulates, organizations can see which judgments are safely delegated and which require override. Third, it supports interoperability across alliances, where different states operate under different legal and regulatory regimes but still need a common structure for coordination.

This matters now because the current environment is already moving by leverage. The Pentagon has acknowledged using its confrontation with Anthropic as leverage in negotiations with other AI providers. That is boundary allocation by power dynamics. It is understandable. It is not design. At the same time, Chinese labs are bypassing boundaries altogether through extraction. That is boundary dissolution. It is predictable. It is also not design.

When boundaries are not designed, they are determined by leverage. And leverage shifts.

So the practical implication is not that we need stronger rhetoric about ethics. Nor is it that we need more admiration for technological sophistication. It is that institutions need explicit architectures for allocating judgment authority before AI systems are deployed into high-stakes domains. That means separating legality from safety from strategic appropriateness, recording decision structure rather than merely decision outcome, and making boundary placement visible, reviewable, and revisable.

AI will not destabilize institutions simply because it is powerful. It will destabilize them because the boundaries governing its judgment remain undesigned, allocated by default, contested by force, and dissolved by extraction.

That is why the alternative to this current confusion is not just better regulation. It is the deliberate design of judgment allocation. And that is where Insynergy’s work with Decision Design becomes relevant: not as a slogan, but as a practical architecture for authority, accountability, and boundary control in AI-augmented organizations.
