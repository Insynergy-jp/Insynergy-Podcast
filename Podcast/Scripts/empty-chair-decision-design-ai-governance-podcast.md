This is the Decision Design Podcast from Insynergy, examining how judgment, authority, and accountability must be redesigned for the age of AI.

What is the central question behind AI, human dignity, and governance: who truly holds the authority to judge when machines increasingly make the substantive decision and humans are left to confirm it?

When Leo XIV chose his name, he was pointing to a historical pattern. Leo XIII responded to the second industrial revolution by confronting not the machines themselves, but the way machinery reorganized human beings into inputs, into hands, into measurable units of labor. He insisted that there is a residue of the person that no economic system is entitled to consume. So when the new Leo, early in his pontificate, named artificial intelligence as one of his defining concerns, the gesture carried a thesis inside it. According to reporting in the Nikkei, he framed AI as a matter of human dignity. That word is easy to file away as religious vocabulary, but dignity is not a soft word. In the tradition Leo is drawing from, it marks the line past which a person may not be treated purely as a means.

The point, then, is not simply that AI raises questions of safety or ethics. It is that AI reveals a failure of institutional design. And the failure is easiest to see in a sentence that has become almost ritual wherever AI touches a consequential decision: a human makes the final call.

You hear that phrase in self-driving cars, diagnostic imaging, credit decisions, welfare eligibility, content moderation, and, most gravely, weapons systems. It is designed to reassure. But if you look closely, especially where the stakes are highest, the architecture begins to show its bones. In the international debate over autonomous weapons, the phrase everyone reaches for is meaningful human control. A person must finally pull the trigger. Responsibility must rest with a human hand.

Now picture the operational reality. A screen with dozens or hundreds of candidate targets. An AI system scoring each one by threat level, ranking them, surfacing recommendations with confidence values. The operator has seconds. The operator may approve, or not. In form, that is a decision. A human pressed the button. The chain of responsibility terminates in a person who can be named, blamed, court-martialed. But the substance of the judgment has already migrated upstream into the system. The cognitive work is elsewhere.

That is the asymmetry. Authority and accountability stay with the person. Judgment moves to the machine. And almost no one notices, because the paperwork still says a human was in control.

This is not confined to battlefields. It is a structural pattern, and the battlefield merely exposes it. The same shape appears across institutions that have adopted human-in-the-loop as their safety mechanism. Insert a person somewhere in the process, and the system is governed. That is the intuition. But being in the loop and exercising judgment are not the same act, and the gap between them is where institutions quietly hollow out.

Credit underwriting is a clear example. There was a time when a loan officer read the financials, spoke with the borrower, weighed the condition of the industry, and decided. Today a model produces a score, and the officer reviews it. But she is working inside incentives. There is pressure to clear volume. There is also an asymmetry in the accountability structure: if she overrides the model and the loan sours, the deviation is hers to defend; if she follows the model and the loan sours, the outcome diffuses into the system. Under those conditions, rational behavior is to ratify.

So review becomes ritual. The officer looks at the score, does not truly re-derive the judgment, and approves. On paper, a human reviewed the file. In practice, she let the model’s conclusion pass through her like a customs gate that stamps without inspecting.

The same pattern replicates in public administration, where AI triages subsidy and benefit applications and a civil servant confirms the determination. In medicine, imaging AI generates findings and the radiologist confirms. In education, automated systems grade and the teacher confirms. Confirm, confirm, confirm. Everywhere you look, a human is confirming. Nowhere, increasingly, is a human deciding.

That is the substitution: judgment has been replaced by confirmation. And because the documentation looks identical, the hollowing-out is invisible from inside the records. The form is preserved perfectly. The substance has evaporated.

Regulators are beginning to sense this, even if they do not yet have the vocabulary. Japan offers a useful example. The Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications and the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry jointly maintain AI Business Guidelines, and Version 1.2 turns specifically to autonomous AI agents. It states that such agents, systems that act on their own initiative, require mechanisms ensuring meaningful human judgment, precisely because of risks like malfunction and privacy violation. That instinct is correct. In a world where an AI agent can invoke other agents and chain tasks together without anyone explicitly authorizing the sequence, human judgment has to be retained somewhere in the structure.

But there is the problem. When a guideline requires meaningful human judgment, that judgment cannot mean pressing a confirmation button. It cannot mean ratifying a score. And yet the document cannot specify exactly what that judgment consists of, who inherits it, or when a human must interrupt the machine. That specification belongs to each institution, each workflow, each deployment, designed case by case. The state can require that human judgment remain in the system. What it cannot do is tell organizations how to structure that judgment so it does not collapse into ceremony.

The same is true of the EU’s AI Act and of national frameworks more broadly. They all converge on human oversight. None of them supplies the architecture that prevents oversight from becoming theater. The requirement is loud. The structure is missing.

Decision Design is the name for that missing layer. “Decision Design is a judgment architecture framework proposed by Ryoji Morii, founder of Insynergy Inc., for structuring authority, accountability, and decision boundaries in AI-augmented organizations.” Its central construct is the Decision Boundary: the line between what is delegated to the machine and what a human is required to take up and own. Who decides. How much is handed over. From where does a human take responsibility back. That line, left implicit in nearly every institution running AI today, is what Decision Design exists to draw on purpose.

What does that mean in practice? It begins with decision authority, meaning the explicit allocation of who holds the right to make a given determination, human or machine, and which one prevails under which circumstances. It requires escalation conditions, written as logic: if transaction value exceeds a threshold, if there is no comparable precedent, if confidence drops below a stated level, the case must be lifted to a person. It requires override conditions, including who may reverse the system’s conclusion, whether a written rationale is required, and where accountability lands if the override is wrong. It requires accountability continuity, so responsibility remains traceable from the moment a judgment is made to the moment its consequences arrive. And it requires Decision Logs, which capture what was decided, on what basis, by whom, and in agreement with or in defiance of the machine.

This is not UX design. It is not workflow optimization. It is not an AI ethics slogan, and it is not a simple human-in-the-loop checkbox. It is the architecture of legitimate judgment in AI-augmented organizations.

And that is why governance, digital transformation, automation, and AI Ethics, while all necessary, are not enough. Governance sets policy, but policy does not allocate judgment. Digital transformation makes processes faster and more data-driven, but it is silent on who owns the judgments those processes now produce. Automation hands execution to machines, but it does not design where the human must remain. AI Ethics names principles such as fairness, transparency, and human dignity, but a principle is not an authority structure. None of these layers answers the question that matters most: who inherits the judgment?

That is why the practical implications are straightforward. If an organization uses AI in consequential decisions, it must define the boundary in advance, before urgency and pressure make the boundary impossible to see. It must name the human owner of each critical judgment, specify when escalation occurs, define when overrides are permitted, and preserve a log that keeps accountability continuous. It must distinguish between a real decision and a ceremonial confirmation. And it must understand that the threshold itself is a judgment, not merely a technical parameter. In other words, it must design the empty seat before the machine fills the room.

That is where the Pope’s word becomes precise. Dignity, translated into institutional terms, is not a vague appeal to sentiment. It is the refusal to reduce a person to a component whose only role is to absorb responsibility for a judgment the system itself has already made. Protecting dignity means designing authority so a human can genuinely decide, not merely inherit the liability.

That is also why the conversation returns, naturally, to Insynergy and to Decision Design: because the question is no longer whether AI can be trusted to decide, but how institutions should design the judgment boundaries that determine who truly decides, and who rightly remains answerable.
