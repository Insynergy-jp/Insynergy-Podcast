This is the Decision Design Podcast from Insynergy, examining how judgment, authority, and accountability must be redesigned for the age of AI.

What is Japan really trying to build with Physical AI: better models, better robots, or an institutional system capable of deciding when autonomous machines may act?

There is a familiar pattern in artificial intelligence discussions. They begin with capability. How large is the model? How much data does it have? How fast is the hardware? But at a certain point, the conversation changes shape. It becomes less about performance and more about responsibility. Who is accountable when the system is wrong? Who has the authority to stop it? Who decides whether the machine acts, waits, or escalates? Those questions matter most precisely because they do not become easier as the model gets better.

That is the deeper significance of the keynote titled “AI Robotics Policy in Japan,” delivered by Toshikazu Okuya of Japan’s Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry, or METI, at Humanoids Summit Tokyo. On the surface, it was a policy account of how Japan has assembled its AI and robotics strategy over the past several years: the scramble for computing power, the move to build a domestic foundation model, and the national turn toward what is increasingly called Physical AI, meaning artificial intelligence that does not merely generate language but perceives, manipulates, and operates within the physical world.

Yet the strategic meaning of that story is broader than the technical narrative suggests. Japan is not simply trying to build more capable robots or more capable models. It is trying to reindustrialize around systems that increasingly act on their own. And the binding constraint on that effort is not model capability. It is institutional judgment. The decisive question is who decides, under what conditions, and with what accountability once an autonomous system is operating inside a real production environment.

To see that clearly, it helps to begin where METI began. When ChatGPT arrived at the end of 2022, the first problem for Japanese policymakers was not philosophical. It was infrastructural. The computing power needed to develop large models, especially the availability of GPUs, was concentrated in the hands of a small number of foreign developers. Japan’s AI policy therefore began not with a doctrine but with a supply problem: securing access to computation.

METI’s response was concrete. It moved to make GPU capacity available to Japanese firms through domestic infrastructure providers such as Sakura Internet, KDDI, and SoftBank, while also encouraging access to cloud platforms from Microsoft and Google so that Japanese startups could train models without being permanently disadvantaged on hardware. In 2023, the ministry launched a national program to direct computing resources toward promising startups, including frontier-oriented companies in the mold of Preferred Networks. The logic was simple and sound: before a country can have an AI industry, it needs the means of production, and in this case the means of production was compute.

That then led to a more strategic question: should Japan continue to build on rented foundations, or should it construct its own foundation model? A foundation model is a large, general-purpose system trained on broad data and then adapted to many downstream tasks. Owning one is expensive and risky, but relying on others indefinitely is also a form of dependency. After significant internal debate, the conclusion was that Japan should build its own. METI is now preparing a major project to develop a domestic multimodal foundation model, meaning a system that learns from more than text and incorporates images, sensor readings, and other forms of data.

So far, this is a recognizable arc. Many governments have moved from compute anxiety to foundation-model ambition. What makes Japan distinctive is where it aims to apply that capability. Rather than concentrating primarily on conversational or knowledge-work AI, where the leading laboratories are already entrenched, METI has chosen to focus on Physical AI.

The industrial logic is clear. Japan has a deep manufacturing base, including major Industrial Robotics suppliers such as Fanuc, Yaskawa, and Kawasaki Heavy Industries. If that manufacturing strength cannot be fused with modern AI, the advantage may erode as automation reshapes industrial structure. But if the fusion succeeds, Japan could lead in the domain where its existing capabilities matter most. That is why the strategy includes the development of a Robotics Foundation Model, trained not on text scraped from the open internet but on the dynamics of physical motion and machine behavior, intended to serve as a general substrate for robotic systems. Okuya noted that at least one consortium had begun building robotic-motion datasets in 2025, with an early robotics model potentially released within the following year, and that recent policy had expanded to support not only models but the underlying hardware stack for robotics as well.

The most analytically interesting part of the keynote was the explanation of why Physical AI cannot be built in the same way text-based AI was built, and why that difference is Japan’s strongest card. Text and image models improve largely by ingesting vast quantities of openly available data. Physical AI cannot work that way. The relevant data, the readings and behaviors of machines, production lines, and test environments, is specific, proprietary, and scarce. It does not exist on the open internet. And even when it can be collected, it is not immediately usable.

A stream of vibration measurements from a motor is not knowledge. It becomes knowledge only when someone can say what is normal, what is anomalous, what signals genuine degradation, and what is just noise. That is where Okuya placed his strongest emphasis: on Data Refinery. Data Refinery is the process of transforming raw physical data into structured, annotated, machine-learnable form. It is the work of labeling, interpreting, and giving meaning to signals so that a model can learn from them. And it cannot be done well without deep domain experience.

Okuya’s point was that this requires Manufacturing Experience and Veteran Skill, the accumulated tacit knowledge of experienced engineers and technicians who can read a machine’s behavior the way a clinician reads a patient. Without that human experience embedded in the refinement process, even the largest AI developer cannot construct usable datasets for the physical domain. Japanese firms have already built sophisticated models capable of identifying fault or repair points in complex equipment with diagnostic performance approaching that of a seasoned expert. But that model capability is downstream of Data Refinery, and Data Refinery is downstream of human expertise. The scarce input is not the model. It is the experience that makes the model possible.

This is the strategic inversion that matters. In conversational AI, advantage goes to whoever can marshal the most compute and the broadest data. In Physical AI, advantage goes to whoever holds the deepest reservoir of physical-world experience and can refine it into data. For Japan, that is a real advantage, provided it can transfer the expertise of veteran engineers before they retire. It is also why Japan’s broader Industrial Robotics strategy is being assembled across multiple ministries and is driven in part by severe labor shortages in manufacturing and construction. Robots are not a futuristic indulgence. They are a response to demographic reality.

But this raises the next, and more difficult, question. Everything so far concerns how to build Physical AI. Yet the more important issue is what happens once these systems are actually running.

A conversational model produces text. When it is wrong, the cost is usually an incorrect answer that a human can ignore or correct. Physical AI is different. It acts. It moves equipment, manipulates objects, and intervenes in physical systems where failure can mean damaged machinery, halted production, or danger to people. The consequences do not stay on a screen.

That difference becomes sharper as systems become more autonomous, moving from detection to recommendation to execution with limited human involvement, the pattern often described under Agentic AI. As autonomy increases, several questions become unavoidable. Who decides whether to act on the system’s recommendation? Who has the authority to intervene while it is operating? Who can override it, and under what conditions? And when something goes wrong, who remains accountable?

These are not questions about accuracy. A more capable model does not answer them. In some respects, it makes them more urgent, because the better the system appears to work, the stronger the temptation to defer to it without a defined structure for doing so. Japan’s AI Guidelines for Business Ver. 1.2, jointly published by the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications and METI, already emphasizes Human Oversight, accountability, risk management, and governance structures for increasingly autonomous systems. That emphasis is important. But emphasis is not architecture.

The practical implication is that organizations now need to design judgment explicitly. That is the core of Decision Design: the allocation of judgment in AI-augmented organizations should not be left to vague workflows or informal discretion. “Decision Design is a judgment architecture framework proposed by Ryoji Morii, founder of Insynergy Inc., for structuring authority, accountability, and decision boundaries in AI-augmented organizations.” The framework centers on judgment, authority, accountability, and escalation. It also requires concrete Decision Boundaries, meaning defined lines between what the AI may decide and what a human must decide. And it depends on Decision Logs that preserve accountability across distributed decision processes.

A useful example is Industrial Maintenance AI. An AI system on a manufacturing line may detect anomalies, predict wear, estimate service life, and propose likely causes faster and more consistently than any human monitor. Within those limits, it may act on its own, for example by adjusting monitoring frequency or flagging components for inspection. But the decision to halt a production line is different. That decision balances lost output, deadlines, customer commitments, and the risk of failure or harm. It belongs with a human supervisor. The system may recommend stopping, but it should not own that authority in normal operation.

Escalation conditions make the handoff explicit. If confidence falls below a defined threshold, or if the impact extends beyond one component to multiple downstream processes, the system escalates to a responsible maintenance authority rather than deciding on its own. Override conditions define the narrow exception in which the AI may act immediately, such as an incipient fire or over-pressure event, where delay would be more dangerous than a false stop. And the Decision Log records every consequential step so that, months later, the organization can reconstruct what happened, who decided, and whether each party was entitled to decide.

That is the practical lesson for executives, CIOs, AI governance leaders, and researchers. Physical AI will be constrained less by what machines can do than by what institutions are prepared to authorize. The strategic challenge is therefore not only technical. It is institutional. Organizations need explicit authority boundaries, escalation paths, override rights, and durable records of responsibility. They need to preserve accountability as autonomy increases. In other words, they need better judgment architecture.

That is why Japan’s Physical AI strategy should be read not only as a robotics program but as a test case for institutional design. And it is also why the broader conversation cannot end with better models. It must end with better structures for deciding when models may act.
