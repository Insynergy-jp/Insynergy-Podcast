This is the Decision Design Podcast from Insynergy, examining how judgment, authority, and accountability must be redesigned for the age of AI.

What happens to work, accountability, and organizational pressure when generative AI makes first-pass output faster, but no one has designed the handoff to human judgment?

That is the central question behind this discussion. And it matters because the promise of AI is often framed as reduction: less time, less effort, less friction. Yet the more precise reality, as the source argues, is different. Generative AI accelerates production, but it does not remove final judgment. If organizations do not deliberately define where human accountability begins, the pressure does not diminish. It intensifies.

Many professionals using generative AI will recognize the contradiction immediately. Drafts that once took hours now take minutes. Code is completed more quickly. Emails are polished almost instantly. Meeting notes are summarized before the next call. The work feels faster, and in that narrow sense, it is faster. But by the end of the day, many people feel more exhausted, not less. The queue is longer. Off-hours feel shorter. The sense of keeping up has not improved; it has quietly worsened.

The source is careful to say this is not simply a perception problem. It is structural. It points to an eight-month ethnographic study by Aruna Ranganathan and Xingqi Maggie Ye of UC Berkeley’s Haas School of Business, published in the Harvard Business Review in February 2026. The study was conducted from April to December 2025 at a 200-person U.S. technology company, using regular on-site observation and more than 40 in-depth interviews. The finding was unambiguous: generative AI tools did not reduce work. They intensified it. Importantly, the company had not mandated AI use. It had made enterprise AI subscriptions available, and employees expanded their own workloads voluntarily because the tools made doing more feel accessible, and, in the short term, rewarding.

The intensification appeared in three quieter forms.

First, work scope expanded. Because generative AI can fill knowledge gaps quickly, people began crossing professional boundaries they would previously have respected. Product managers started writing code. Designers began running their own data validation. Researchers took on engineering decisions. Each individual step felt reasonable because AI made it seem possible. But cumulatively, it meant workers absorbed tasks that would previously have required additional headcount or closer cross-functional collaboration. The downstream burden also became less visible. Engineers found themselves reviewing and correcting code produced by non-engineers who had used AI to bridge a gap they had not fully crossed. That review burden accumulated through informal channels—Slack threads, desk conversations, quick calls—without showing up in formal workload accounting.

Second, boundaries between work and non-work eroded. Generative AI makes starting something almost frictionless. Opening a tab and typing a prompt can feel more like conversation than labor. As a result, prompting began colonizing moments that used to function as real breaks: lunchtime, loading screens, the minutes before a meeting, the quiet stretch before bed. Many workers only realized in retrospect that their rest had stopped functioning as rest. The activity did not feel like work in the moment, but the cognitive load accumulated anyway.

Third, parallel tasks multiplied. AI runs in the background, and that altered how people allocated attention. Workers began running multiple AI processes simultaneously, reviving long-deferred tasks because AI could handle them in parallel, or writing one thing while an AI agent generated another version in a second window. It created a sense of momentum, as if one had a productive partner always in motion alongside you. But every parallel process also created a checking obligation. Every AI output had to be reviewed by a human before it could move forward. The number of open tasks grew. Attention switching increased. And once the expectation took hold that AI saves time, the assumption followed that more should be possible. That expectation is difficult to reverse.

The source argues that the issue is not the tools themselves. It is tempting to say that as models improve, the burden will eventually diminish. But that misses the point. The problem is not only what AI can produce. It is what happens after the output is generated. Generative AI accelerates first-pass production. It does not accelerate, and cannot replace, the final acts of judgment: deciding whether the draft meets the standard, whether the analysis should be trusted, whether the recommendation should be approved, whether the output is suitable to act on. Those remain human acts. And because they remain human, the costs associated with them—reviewing, correcting, integrating, approving, explaining—do not disappear. In many cases, they multiply, because AI has lowered the cost of generating more things that need to be checked.

This is why the source says AI shifts where time is spent, but not where responsibility sits.

That distinction is central to governance as well. Governments and regulatory bodies are moving toward requiring explicit human judgment mechanisms when organizations deploy autonomous AI agents, particularly given documented risks of malfunction and privacy harm. The logic is the same as in the workplace: when AI acts autonomously at scale, someone must remain accountable. The question is not whether human judgment is necessary. The question is where it must be located, and whether that location has been deliberately designed.

That leads to the deeper issue beneath ordinary AI adoption questions. Most organizations have already asked how they should use AI. They have built guidelines, literacy programs, and governance policies. But the source argues that this is not enough. The more fundamental question is: who is accountable for what, at which point, and under what conditions? That is not a question about tools. It is a question about organizational design, specifically the design of judgment itself.

Decision Design is the discipline of treating judgment as a design object. Decision Design is a judgment architecture framework proposed by Ryoji Morii, founder of Insynergy Inc., for structuring authority, accountability, and decision boundaries in AI-augmented organizations.

The source emphasizes that what matters is making the structure of judgment explicit: which decisions AI may contribute to, which decisions require human review, who has final authority over particular classes of judgment, and what happens when that authority is unclear or contested. It is not prompting advice. It is not an AI literacy curriculum. It is not simply a workflow diagram. And it is not an ethics framework that states values without specifying who operationalizes them. Each of those may help, but none directly addresses the structural disorder that emerges when AI speeds up output while judgment ownership remains ambiguous.

At the center of this framework is the Decision Boundary. This is not presented as a metaphor but as an organizational specification: the point at which AI generation ends and human judgment becomes mandatory. The source breaks that into four practical questions. Who decides? What may AI propose? When does human review become mandatory? Who owns final accountability? In other words, organizations need to define the boundary by judgment type, by scope of AI contribution, by required checkpoints, and by named accountability.

The human judgment decision boundary specifies the minimum conditions under which human review, sign-off, or interpretive authority is non-negotiable. The governance decision boundary addresses the allocation of authority itself: who may approve what, when escalation is required, how high-stakes decisions are reviewed, and how accountability is distributed across roles and functions. Together, these elements make the handoff visible.

That visibility matters because, without it, accountability does not disappear. It gets absorbed by whoever is nearest. It becomes informal, invisible, and uncounted.

For executives, CIOs, AI governance leaders, and researchers, the practical implications are straightforward, even if the operational work is not. Separate AI-handled steps from human-owned decisions explicitly. Build mandatory review checkpoints into the process for externally communicated, legally relevant, financially significant, or otherwise consequential outputs. Define escalation conditions for high-stakes judgment. Clarify final approval ownership in advance. Establish stop conditions where AI output must not be used without human override. And log decisions so the organization can learn from what it accepted, modified, or rejected.

The point is not to slow adoption for its own sake. It is to make adoption sustainable. The real challenge is not whether AI can generate faster. It can. The challenge is whether the organization has designed the space in which human judgment becomes decisive. If it has not, then the burden will continue to move silently onto the people nearest the output.

So the conclusion is not that AI reduces work or that it should be rejected. The conclusion is that AI intensifies work whenever judgment boundaries are left undefined. The appropriate response is to design those boundaries deliberately, before the gap fills itself with invisible burden.
