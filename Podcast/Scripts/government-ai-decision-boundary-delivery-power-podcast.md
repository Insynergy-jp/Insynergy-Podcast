This is the Decision Design Podcast from Insynergy, examining how judgment, authority, and accountability must be redesigned for the age of AI.

The central question is whether Japan’s government can turn AI into delivery power without losing clarity over who decides, who is accountable, and where human judgment must begin.

In February 2026, at a joint career meetup hosted by Japan’s Digital Agency and GovTech Tokyo, Tokyo Vice-Governor Manabu Miyasaka made a deceptively simple observation. Amazon did not write books. It made books arrive faster. Uber did not invent the car ride. Netflix did not invent film. In each case, the product stayed the same, but the power to deliver changed. And when that power multiplied tenfold, entire industries were remade.

What made that observation especially consequential was the setting. Miyasaka was not speaking about startups or markets. He was speaking about government. Imagine applying for a grant in the morning and receiving support by evening. Imagine government proactively pushing notifications that say, “There’s a support program for you,” instead of waiting for citizens to navigate opaque bureaucracies. That, he argued, is the real promise of digital transformation in the public sector. Yet the present reality still includes thick paper applications, in-person counters, and reviews that take six months. Politicians design policy. What is missing is the power to deliver that policy to citizens at speed.

That is the tension that now matters. As delivery accelerates, judgment must accelerate as well. And as judgment speeds up, accountability can become dangerously blurry. Japan’s national government and Tokyo Metropolitan Government are both racing to deploy AI at scale, but the harder question is whether they are designing the boundaries of judgment with the same urgency.

On the national side, Shingo Yamaguchi, who oversees AI implementation strategy at the Digital Agency, reported on the rollout of Gennai, the Government AI platform, accessed through the government employee portal. Gennai currently hosts roughly 230 applications, from general-purpose tools such as chat and translation to specialized apps that use retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG, against administrative documents. It was launched inside the Digital Agency in May 2025 and is now budgeted for deployment to more than 100,000 government employees by May 2026.

One notable feature is that Gennai is built internally. About 25 engineers, most recruited from the private sector, work alongside product managers and designers. When a new model is released, it is integrated almost immediately. That pace is fundamentally alien to Japan’s traditional government procurement culture, where system updates typically require multi-year cycles.

In late 2025, Prime Minister Takaichi issued seven directives on AI, and the first mandated the expansion of Gennai to more than 100,000 government employees. The directive was blunt: senior officials must use it, increase their fluency, and apply AI creatively in their work. As Yamaguchi noted, having top-down commitment at that level is fortunate, because it lets the team move quickly without the usual bureaucratic friction.

Tokyo’s strategy, as described by Masataka Tsuji of the Digital Services Bureau, takes a different but complementary form. The metropolitan government formally adopted its AI strategy in July 2025, following the work of an AI strategy council that included University of Tokyo professor Yutaka Matsuo. Its core architecture is a traffic-light classification model. Tokyo’s operations are divided into three domains: services where citizens interact directly with AI, services where employees use AI to process citizen-facing work, and purely internal employee workflows. Each domain is then assessed across five levels of AI maturity and color-coded. Green means aggressive adoption. Yellow means proceed with risk awareness. Red means wait. Internal employee workflows are entirely green.

Tsuji emphasized something that matters more than it might first appear: AI adoption must not become the objective itself. It has to remain a means. In fiscal year 2026, AI-related projects across Tokyo’s government increased roughly 80% year over year, exceeding 200 initiatives, with infrastructure and urban planning accounting for about a quarter. The point is not experimentation for its own sake. It is whether AI makes policy delivery faster, fairer, and more accountable.

Some of the most concrete results are already visible in work that would have been slow, repetitive, and highly manual under conventional methods. Yamaguchi described a collaboration with the Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries after the rice shortage problem. MAFF conducted a nationwide survey of rice farmers: 30 items, 8,000 responses. Under ordinary conditions, a single analyst would spend two to three months cross-tabulating the data in Excel.

The division of labor here is revealing. MAFF focuses on forming hypotheses, such as whether young rice farmers in mountainous regions plan to increase or decrease planting area next season. The Digital Agency then takes those hypotheses, feeds them with survey data into AI, and returns validated reports. The implication is that human work changes. Humans concentrate on hypothesis formation and stakeholder communication. Data analysis is offloaded to AI. And if this use case spreads, the bureaucracy may begin to recognize that there is a better way to work.

Tokyo has also begun piloting AI-assisted grant and subsidy screening through its generative AI platform. Grant administration is a core metropolitan function, and each application requires painstaking manual review. Here again, the structure matters. Formal screening, meaning the checking of eligibility requirements, can be handled by AI with high reliability. Substantive review, meaning the evaluation of business plan feasibility, regional economic impact, and applicant capacity, raises hard questions about where AI authority ends. And final judgment, under Tokyo’s strategy, must remain with a human decision-maker.

Formal screening, substantive review, final human decision. This is not just an operational workflow. It is boundary design. It defines where AI stops and human accountability begins. Yamaguchi made the same point from the national side. Formal screening can be delegated to AI. But edge cases will arise. Government needs a world where, in those gray zones, officials can use AI as a sparring partner while retaining the authority to judge.

Japan is also moving to require AI developers to build mechanisms mandating human judgment when autonomous AI agents are deployed. That regulatory instinct and the internal operational design are part of the same conversation. When government designs AI judgment boundaries within its own operations, it is extracting the basis for regulation from its own practice.

Sota Mizushima, the Digital Agency’s CPO, framed the transition clearly. 2025 was a year of trial and error. The public sector was keeping pace with the private sector in AI experimentation, but whether real outcomes were being produced remained unclear. It was a year of touching things and seeing what happens. Now it is 2026. “We tried it” is no longer a sufficient answer. Gennai is moving to more than 100,000 employees. Tokyo has more than 200 AI projects underway. Grant screening automation has entered pilot. The question is no longer how many hours AI saved. It is how the line between AI judgment and human judgment was drawn.

That is the key practical issue. The real challenge is not adoption, but who decides and who is accountable. If formal screening is delegated to AI, substantive review becomes the frontier, and final judgment remains human, then the organization must say explicitly who drew those boundaries and on what principles. Otherwise the layers collapse. The human reviewer rubber-stamps the AI output. The final decision-maker ratifies without scrutiny. The boundary exists on paper but not in practice.

This is why the most important design work is not the model, and not the prompt. It is the boundary between AI and human judgment. “Decision Design is a judgment architecture framework proposed by Ryoji Morii, founder of Insynergy Inc., for structuring authority, accountability, and decision boundaries in AI-augmented organizations.” In practical terms, that means defining what AI must not do as clearly as what it may do; cataloging gray-zone cases in advance; creating escalation paths with structured information; preventing final approval from becoming ceremonial; logging the relationship between AI output and human decisions; naming the individual accountable at each layer; and reviewing boundary design periodically, not just model performance.

The practical implication for executives, CIOs, AI governance leaders, and researchers is straightforward. If you deploy AI into decision-sensitive workflows, you must design the interface between machine output and human authority with the same rigor you apply to the technology itself. You need explicit negative scope, pre-defined escalation, visible rationales, and named accountability. Otherwise efficiency gains may be real, but trust will remain fragile.

Japan’s national government and Tokyo Metropolitan Government are moving quickly. Gennai is expanding. Tokyo’s AI strategy is operational. Grant screening is being piloted. But the deeper challenge is now visible. When delivery power changes, society changes. Between policy and delivery, there is always a judgment. If that judgment boundary is left undesigned, no one can truly guarantee what gets delivered, or on whose authority.

That is why the central task is not merely to adopt AI, but to govern it as a decision structure. And that is exactly where Insynergy, through Decision Design, enters the conversation.
