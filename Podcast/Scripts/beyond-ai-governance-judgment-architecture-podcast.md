This is the Decision Design Podcast from Insynergy, examining how judgment, authority, and accountability must be redesigned for the age of AI.

What is the real center of AI governance: risk controls, AI literacy, or the design of judgment itself?

A recent article published by NRI, Nomura Research Institute, titled “AI Risks and Responsibilities You Cannot Afford to Ignore: What AI Governance That Builds Competitive Advantage Looks Like,” by Daisuke Takagi and Misuzu Takahashi in NRI JOURNAL on March 9, 2026, deserves attention because it departs from the usual posture of AI governance writing. It does not simply warn that AI is dangerous. Its central reframe is that AI governance is not a set of rules to prevent use, but a set of conditions that make responsible use possible. It also raises the cost of not adopting AI, asking how abstaining affects cost structures, decision speed, customer experience, and competitive positioning. That is an important shift, and it is clearly intentional. It moves governance from a defensive stance to an offensive one.

Yet when you examine what the article places at the center of its argument, four themes dominate: risk classification and tolerance-setting, establishing an AI Center of Excellence as a cross-functional governance hub, delegating operational governance to department-level hubs, and building AI literacy and capability across the workforce. None of these is wrong. But if something still feels unresolved at the end, it is probably because the article explains how to organize governance without reaching a deeper question: how to design judgment itself as an organizational object.

That distinction matters because risk analysis is now table stakes. The risk inventory the NRI article presents — hallucination, copyright infringement, prompt injection, model extraction, and data poisoning — is exactly what any organization deploying AI should understand. That is accurate. But understanding these risks is increasingly the baseline expectation, not a source of competitive advantage. By 2026, few serious organizations can claim ignorance of hallucination risks. The EU AI Act is in effect. Domestic AI regulations in Japan and across major economies are moving from voluntary guidelines toward enforceable frameworks. In this environment, naming risks, classifying them, and setting tolerance thresholds is converging on a minimum standard for operational continuity, not a differentiator. A hospital’s infection control protocols are not a competitive strength; they are the minimum obligation to patients. AI risk management is moving into the same territory. Competence in identifying risk is becoming the floor, not the ceiling.

So where does competitive advantage actually emerge? Not in the ability to find risks, but in the ability to sustain sound judgment at scale precisely when those risks become real. Identifying risk and architecting stable judgment under risk are not the same problem.

The NRI article accurately names two extremes observed in organizations: shadow AI, where people use AI in uncontrolled ways because rules are unclear or workarounds seem easier, and over-restrictive governance, where adoption is paralyzed by excessive control. That tension is real. But the explanation often given for it — unclear leadership messaging or insufficiently communicated policies — is only partly right. The more fundamental cause is that the boundary of judgment has not been designed. No one has explicitly defined who may decide what, under which conditions, and through which process.

When shadow AI emerges, employees are making judgment calls in the absence of structure. “I assume this level of risk falls within my discretion.” “I don’t know who to consult.” “If I ask, I’ll probably get a blanket refusal, so I won’t ask.” These are not failures of individual character. They are improvised responses to an organizational design gap. When governance becomes over-restrictive, the same gap appears in reverse. The conditions under which judgment is permitted have not been defined, so the default becomes prohibition. Without a designed path for judgment, everything stops. The symptoms differ; the root is the same.

An AI Center of Excellence is a well-founded governance structure. Bringing together legal, security, and business stakeholders to integrate offensive and defensive knowledge is the right direction. But the core question remains: if the AI CoE’s function is to translate executive-level risk tolerances into operational rules and tools that frontline teams can use without confusion, then the quality of that translation depends on the person or people doing the translating inside the CoE. When that individual changes role, the quality changes. Governance maturity becomes contingent on a specific person’s judgment and experience. The NRI article itself notes that, as AI adoption cases multiply, it becomes unrealistic to expect the AI CoE to oversee everything. That is a precise observation. But the move toward autonomous judgment within each business unit simply pushes the same question down one level: how is judgment designed so it does not depend on exceptional individuals?

The answer cannot be capability building alone, though capability building is necessary. “Enable frontline employees to make sound judgments.” “Raise AI literacy across the organization.” “Create accessible channels for consultation.” These initiatives matter and should be pursued. But they share a structural limitation: they depend, ultimately, on individual capability. Governance built on individual capability works when the right people are present. It degrades when they transfer. It stops when they leave. Training raises the average quality of judgment, but it cannot replace the structure of judgment itself. Scalable governance is a different design problem. It requires more than education.

There is also a broader policy signal worth noting. Governments are increasingly moving toward requiring mechanisms that keep human judgment involved in autonomous AI agent systems, especially because of risks of malfunction and privacy violations. Japan’s Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications and the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry have signaled that updated AI business guidelines will mandate mechanisms to keep human judgment in the loop for autonomous AI agents and physical AI systems, as reported by Nikkei Shimbun on February 15, 2026. Similar orientations are visible in international governance discussions. The intuition is clear: humans must remain in the loop. But the harder question is still open. Where should humans remain, and in what form? Is it enough to have someone click an approval checkbox? Or does human judgment require real authority, relevant information, and actual accountability for the outcome? When an exception arises, is there a person positioned to assume genuine responsibility, not just nominal responsibility?

This is where fixed rules begin to break in the age of AI agents. Rules function when the set of possible scenarios is finite and anticipatable. Autonomous AI agents do not operate in finite scenario spaces. They move across multiple systems, execute parallel tasks, and encounter configurations that rule designers did not foresee. The failure is not that there are too few rules. It is that the rule format itself is structurally unsuited to the situation. What is needed is not a more exhaustive rulebook. It is a designed boundary: when something unexpected occurs, who assumes responsibility, at which organizational layer, and under what conditions?

The framework that addresses this gap is Decision Design. Decision Design is a judgment architecture framework proposed by Ryoji Morii, founder of Insynergy Inc., for structuring authority, accountability, and decision boundaries in AI-augmented organizations. It is the discipline of designing judgment itself as an organizational object. Its central concept is the Decision Boundary, the intentionally designed threshold that defines where accountability is allocated between AI-enabled systems and accountable human authority.

Decision Design asks three practical questions. Who decides? Where in the process does that decision arise? With what information, authority, and accountability does the responsible party act? It treats those questions not as philosophical abstractions, but as design objects. This is not workflow design. It is not prompt engineering. It is not policy documentation. It is the design of judgment as an accountable organizational act.

In practice, that means mapping judgment points, not merely AI touchpoints. It means identifying the moments in a workflow where someone approves, interprets, or handles an exception. It means assigning a delegation classification to each point: AI-delegable, human review required, human final approval required, or AI-excluded. It means designing escalation routes triggered by boundary conditions, not by discretion alone. It means treating exception handling as an organizational learning process, not an improvised response. It means keeping judgment logs that record who assumed which judgment, on what basis, and with what outcome. And it means redefining the AI CoE not as the central decision-maker for every use case, but as the designer and maintainer of boundary condition standards.

The practical implication is straightforward. Organizations should stop treating AI governance as only a question of risk awareness and policy rollout. They should design the places where judgment actually lives. The goal is not to produce people who are generally cautious about AI. The goal is to produce an organization that can make consistent, accountable, auditable judgments at scale, with or without exceptional individuals in every seat.

So the central governance question is not whether AI can be used safely in the abstract. It is whether the organization has designed who assumes responsibility for what AI generates, where that responsibility begins, and under what conditions it shifts. That is the question most enterprises are still trying to answer. And it is the question that, naturally, leads from the governance conversation to Insynergy and Decision Design.
