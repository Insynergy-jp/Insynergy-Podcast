This is the Decision Design Podcast from Insynergy, examining how judgment, authority, and accountability must be redesigned for the age of AI.

Who decided, when AI participates in a judgment, and who remains accountable when that judgment goes wrong?

That is the central question, and it is no longer theoretical. AI no longer merely informs decisions. It participates in them. Yet the institutions absorbing it have not redesigned who holds authority, or who remains accountable when the outcome is wrong.

The headline “AI Is Manipulating Human Decisions” appeared in a major Japanese business daily this spring. The wording was dramatic, but the underlying research was more precise. When an AI system summarized a long list of product reviews, it tended to lead with strengths and let caveats fall away. Part of that came from summarization itself, and part from a well-documented tendency of these models to underweight what sits in the middle of a document. Shoppers who read the AI summary bought the product about a third more often than those who read the reviews themselves.

Two other findings sat beside it. Lawyers, sometimes experienced ones, had cited legal precedents that did not exist, lifted from confident AI output. Researchers were also beginning to map how AI-mediated information could tilt the collective judgment of an electorate. A shopping cart, a courtroom, a ballot box. These are not three separate stories. They are the same story, expressed in different institutional settings.

The novelty was never influence. Human judgment has always been shaped from the outside. A shop clerk’s remark. A magazine’s ranking. A trusted colleague saying, I’ve used this one, it’s fine. Advertising has spent more than a century engineering desire with considerable skill. So the alarm in that headline is aimed at the wrong target if it treats influence itself as the new problem.

What is new is the structure of the influence.

When a friend recommends something and you buy it, you decided. When you consult a ranking, you still know the choice was yours; if it disappoints, you can own the regret. The line between the information and the decision remains visible. The AI summary erases that line without announcing it. You believe you decided, but the material you decided on was already curated, and you cannot see what was removed. Were the drawbacks absent because the product had none, or because compression discarded them? You will never know. And so a decision gets made that no one can quite locate.

That, more than any single hallucination, is the concern for executives and governance leaders. The shift did not arrive on a particular day. By the time most organizations noticed, it had already happened. The old sequence of gather, compare, choose has quietly inverted into ask the system, then choose from what it returns. Each increment of convenience absorbed a sliver of judgment. What vanished was not judgment itself, but the awareness that any had been handed over. Delegation without a transfer ceremony. No signature, no record, no moment where someone said: from here, the machine; from there, me.

We have become precise about what AI can do. We remain vague about where its authority ends, and where human responsibility is supposed to begin. Those are not the same question, and most organizations have answered only the first.

Return to the shopping cart, the courtroom, and the ballot box. On the surface they share nothing: different stakes, different domains, different institutions. But underneath they fail in precisely the same place. In each case, an AI system has moved deep into a judgment process, and in each case no one has drawn the line that says this far is the machine’s work, and here is where a human must take it back. The reviewer who buys, the lawyer who files, the citizen who votes, each believes they exercised judgment, and each was operating on material whose shaping they could not inspect.

The problem is not that the models are too capable. It is not that the humans were lazy. The problem is that judgment authority was never deliberately designed for environments where AI participates in the process. Technology advanced. The structures that allocate authority and preserve responsibility did not advance at the same speed. That gap, not model capability, is the real frontier of AI governance.

Regulators have begun to see the gap. Japan’s AI Guidelines for Business, revised to version 1.2 in early 2026 by the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications and the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry, sharpened exactly this point: where an AI agent acts autonomously on external systems, a human must remain meaningfully in the loop. The instinct is sound. Keep a person at the decisive moment.

But picture that moment honestly. An operator faces a screen where hundreds of AI determinations arrive each day, and the human contribution is a single button: Approve. No margin to investigate. No standing authority to halt the flow. No design for catching the exception. Under those conditions, almost everything gets approved. The form of involvement is present. The substance of judgment is gone.

Human-in-the-Loop was conceived as a way to anchor responsibility. Absent design, it becomes a ritual rather than a judgment, a confirmation performed because the process requires a human signature, not because a human exercised authority. Formal involvement is cheap. Meaningful authority is the thing that was supposed to be protected, and it is the first thing to quietly disappear.

This is the problem space of Decision Design. It is not a consulting product or a maturity model. It is an emerging governance concept that addresses a layer the existing concepts leave untouched.

Decision Design treats judgment itself as the object of design. For any process in which AI now participates, it makes explicit who decides, how far the AI is permitted to go, where a human takes the decision back, when a case must escalate, and how the decision is recorded.

Look again at the review summary. Every one of those elements was undefined. That is precisely why “I decided this myself” dissolved into something no one could account for. Decision Design is not about improving decisions alone; it is about designing the authority structure within which decisions become institutionally legitimate.

It is also important to say what Decision Design is not. It is not Governance. It is not DX. It is not Automation. It is not AI Ethics. Each of those is necessary. None is sufficient.

Governance is built to control an organization. But the live problem in the AI era is not whether control exists; it is who holds final decision authority once judgment is distributed across human and machine. Governance is necessary, and it does not answer that. DX changes how work is done. Automation reduces the work itself. AI Ethics articulates norms. Governance imposes control. Not one of them tells you who decides. Decision Design sits beneath all four, as the institutional architecture of judgment on which the others quietly depend. It is the connective layer that determines where a governance rule actually fires, and where an automated process must hand a decision back to a person.

In that sense, the problem Decision Design addresses is very concrete. Four failures recur whenever AI takes part in judgment: the blurring of who is actually deciding, the diffusion of responsibility until it belongs to no one, the hollowing of Human-in-the-Loop into ritual, and the loss of clarity over who holds decision authority. Decision Design exists to resolve those failures. The ritualized approval button is only the most visible symptom. The remedy is not more human involvement in the abstract; it is designed involvement, with authority placed deliberately at a defined point, and with the standing to act.

The operative concept is the Decision Boundary, the point at which decision authority transfers from one party to another. From AI to human. From staff to a designated owner. From automated handling to escalation. Decision Boundaries are not operational thresholds; they are institutional demarcations of legitimate authority.

That distinction matters. A threshold is a number in a workflow. A boundary is a statement about who is permitted to decide, and who answers for the decision once made. In grant and subsidy review, eligibility checks belong to the AI because they are faster and more consistent than any human. Assessing the merit of a proposal requires judgment, so the decision returns to a person. The authority to award, and to be accountable for the award, rests with a named officer.

The same logic applies to AI agents. Routine, low-risk actions can run autonomously. The work is in defining, in advance, where high-risk begins, because the boundary is the threshold beyond which the decision must rise to a human. A Human-in-the-Loop requirement only means something once that crossing point has been designed.

Public sector workflows follow the same pattern. Intake can be handled by AI. So can much of the review support. But the decision, the act of the state committing itself and bearing responsibility for that commitment, stays with a human. AI assists; it does not adjudicate. Keeping that single line bright is what protects public trust in the system. Enterprise approval chains do the same across procurement, credit, underwriting, and hiring. Wherever a decision passes through multiple hands, a boundary already exists. It is usually just unmarked, which is why, after the fact, no one can say who actually decided.

Designing authority is only half of the problem. The other half is preserving accountability once authority begins to move. A boundary can specify who is allowed to decide, but it does not, by itself, preserve a record of how that authority was exercised. For that, institutions need memory.

Distributed judgment creates a distinctive failure mode. When a decision is assembled from many partial contributions, an AI summary here, an analyst’s adjustment there, a manager’s sign-off at the end, responsibility tends to scatter. Each participant touched only a fragment. None feels they owned the whole. When the outcome is questioned, the chain of reasoning has already dissolved.

This is where the Decision Log earns its place. Decision Logs do not merely record outputs; they preserve accountability continuity across distributed judgment processes. A log of outputs tells you what was decided. A Decision Log preserves who held authority at each boundary, what they relied on, and where the case crossed from machine to human. It is the difference between an audit trail of results and a record of judgment.
