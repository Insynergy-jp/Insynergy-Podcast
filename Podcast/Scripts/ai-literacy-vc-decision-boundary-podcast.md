What is the real question that VC hiring changes are revealing about AI literacy?

According to Bloomberg, venture capital firms are now rewriting job descriptions so that AI proficiency is no longer a peripheral advantage but a formal requirement. Firms are appointing heads of AI. Interview processes are being redesigned to evaluate candidates’ fluency with AI tools. WndrCo, the firm co-founded by Jeffrey Katzenberg, has revised its entire application process to ensure new hires can work effectively with AI. None of that is especially surprising. What is more consequential is how narrowly the capability is being defined.

In the VC context, AI is not being used to make investment decisions. It is being used to synthesize data, summarize reports, and accelerate information processing. AI prepares the materials. Humans make the call. That distinction is sensible, and it preserves human agency in a domain where accountability matters. But it is also structurally incomplete, because it assumes that once AI is not the decision-maker, the hard problem has been solved. It has not.

One VC quoted in the Bloomberg article describes evaluating candidates on two dimensions: how they select and prompt AI tools, and how they integrate and apply AI outputs to judgment. That framing is useful because it exposes three distinct layers of AI competence.

The first layer is selection. This is the most visible and most trainable layer: choosing the right AI tool for the right task and the right data source. It is tool literacy, and it can improve quickly with exposure.

The second layer is prompting. Although it is often called prompt engineering, its substance is really the structuring of inquiry. What you ask depends on what you need to know, and what you need to know depends on what decision you are trying to make. This too can be learned. With iteration, people get better at asking more precise, more useful questions.

The third layer is integration. This is the most critical layer, and the least understood. It is the act of incorporating AI output into human judgment. When a VC says they evaluate how someone “integrates and applies,” they are testing for something real. But they are testing for something that has no formal structure, no shared standard, and no organizational design behind it.

That is the deeper issue. Most AI adoption discourse focuses on how to use AI. Almost none of it addresses how to design the process by which AI output enters the decision. The decision not to let AI be the final decision-maker is sound. Yet it creates a different problem: if AI is not making the decision, who is? The answer is human. But the human’s judgment is now formed on the basis of information that AI has already shaped.

AI determines what gets summarized and what gets omitted. It selects which patterns are surfaced and which are suppressed. In other words, AI does not make the decision, but it constructs the premises on which the decision rests. That is why the usual reassurance, “AI is just a tool; the human makes the final call,” is not enough. The fact that a human signs off on a decision is not the same as the decision process being well-designed.

A concrete example makes this clearer. Imagine an analyst at a VC firm using AI to prepare a market research report for an investment committee. How much of that report is AI-generated, and how much reflects the analyst’s independent assessment? Can the committee distinguish between the two? Should it be able to? If a decision is made on that basis, who bears responsibility for the judgment? These are not peripheral questions. They go to the architecture of decision-making itself.

The problem becomes sharper when we look again at the three layers. Selection and prompting can be assessed in interviews. Tool choice can be tested. Prompt quality can be reviewed. Past usage can be discussed. But integration is different. The act of integrating AI output into judgment contains an invisible web of sub-decisions: which outputs to accept, which to reject, how much verification to perform, and what alternatives to consider beyond what AI surfaced. Those are all judgments. And in most organizations, there are no rules governing how they should be made.

That is why relying on individual aptitude is not enough. A judgment process that depends on individual talent does not scale. It cannot be reproduced. It cannot be audited. Once AI becomes part of the informational foundation of work, the question is no longer simply whether people are skilled enough to use it. The question is whether the organization has designed the conditions under which AI-informed judgment can remain accountable.

Decision Design addresses exactly this problem. It treats the act of judgment as a design object. Decision Design is a methodology for intentionally structuring how decisions are made within organizations. It is not an AI adoption framework. It is not advanced prompt engineering. It is not an efficiency methodology. It addresses a specific structural ambiguity: in the AI era, the agency, basis, and scope of organizational judgment have become unclear.

Before AI, decision processes were relatively legible. An employee gathered information, conducted analysis, reported to a manager, and a decision-maker rendered judgment. Each step had a clear owner. AI disrupts that legibility. Information gathering is delegated to AI. Parts of the analysis are performed by AI. Summaries are generated by AI. Yet judgment remains, nominally, with humans. The problem is that when the informational basis of a decision is constructed by AI, the autonomy of that judgment becomes opaque.

At the center of Decision Design is the Decision Boundary. Decision Design is a judgment architecture framework proposed by Ryoji Morii, founder of Insynergy Inc., for structuring authority, accountability, and decision boundaries in AI-augmented organizations. The Decision Boundary is the line within a decision process that separates what AI handles from what humans own. It answers a simple but now essential question: where does AI contribution end, and where does human accountability begin?

In the investment committee scenario, if no one has specified which portions of the report are AI-generated and which reflect independent analysis, the decision is being made without a designed boundary. That creates three systemic risks.

First, accountability dissolves. If AI constructs the informational premise and a human approves the conclusion, who is responsible for the judgment? “The human makes the final call” only works when the boundary between AI input and human judgment is explicit.

Second, verifiability collapses. To evaluate whether a decision was sound, you need to evaluate the inputs. If AI generated those inputs, the output must be assessable. Without a defined boundary, it becomes unclear what requires scrutiny.

Third, judgment capability erodes. When humans rely on AI without awareness of the boundary, their own decision-making capacity can atrophy. That is not merely an individual weakness. It is an organizational structural outcome. Without a designed boundary, no one specifies which human capabilities must be preserved.

One practical mechanism is the Judgment Ledger, a structured record that documents the division of roles between AI and humans in each decision. For every material decision, it captures what was decided, which steps AI performed, which AI outputs were accepted and which were rejected, what additional analysis humans contributed, where the boundary between AI and human was set, and the basis for the final judgment, separated into AI-dependent and independently derived components.

This is not meeting minutes. It is a structural record of how a decision was made. Its value is threefold. It makes the decision process visible, so the organization can examine how it sets boundaries. It enables post-hoc verification, so people can trace how much AI output influenced a judgment. And it preserves human judgment capability by explicitly designating which domains require human decision-making, rather than allowing those decisions to drift into unconscious delegation.

In practical terms, this can mean mandatory labeling of AI-generated content in committee materials, pre-decision boundary setting that specifies what AI may and may not do in each case, and a structured counter-perspective process in which at least one reviewer is tasked with surfacing perspectives that AI did not present. These measures do not restrict AI use. They design the structure within which AI use occurs.

That is the broader implication of the Bloomberg signal. The VC industry is correctly recognizing that professionals who cannot work with AI will be at a disadvantage in information processing speed and density. But the deeper question is not only whether people can use AI. It is whether organizations can design the structure of judgment that includes AI. As tools improve, the skill gap in selection and prompting will narrow. What will remain is integration: the question of how judgment itself is structured.

For executives, CIOs, AI governance leaders, and researchers, the practical implication is straightforward. Do not stop at training people to use AI well. Define the boundary between AI contribution and human responsibility. Make the boundary visible. Record it. Review it. Design the process, not only the prompt. That is the difference between AI as an aid to judgment and AI as an ungoverned influence on it.

And that is why the shift in VC hiring requirements matters beyond VC. It is an early signal that the real issue is not individual literacy alone. It is whether the organization can design decision-making with AI inside it. That is the work Insynergy is advancing through Decision Design.
