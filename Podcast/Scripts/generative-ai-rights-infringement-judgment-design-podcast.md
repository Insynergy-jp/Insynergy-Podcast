This is the Decision Design Podcast from Insynergy, examining how judgment, authority, and accountability must be redesigned for the age of AI.

What is Japan’s Ministry of Justice really asking when it convenes an expert panel on generative AI rights infringement? On the surface, it is asking a legal question about publicity rights, voice rights, deepfake pornography, and civil tort liability. But at a deeper level, it is asking a question about judgment design: who decides, on what basis, and within what boundary, when AI systems create harm faster than human organizations can absorb it.

In 2026, according to reporting by the Asahi Shimbun, Japan’s Ministry of Justice began the legal organization of rights-infringement issues arising from generative AI, with a consolidated set of findings expected in July. Read narrowly, this is a familiar move. Legal systems do what legal systems do: they extend existing doctrinal categories to a new technological setting. How far does voice protection extend? When does a resembling face become an infringement? What remedy is available when a deepfake causes harm? These are unavoidable questions, and they will require legal answers.

But if we stop there, we miss the structural shift. Generative AI rights infringement differs from prior forms of infringement in three ways that matter operationally. First, the speed of infringement production now far exceeds the speed of human moderation. Second, attribution is unstable. Is the infringing actor the user who wrote the prompt, the company that provided the model, the party that supplied the training data, or some combination? Third, the boundary of infringement is continuous rather than discrete. Between “resembles,” “looks like,” and “is identical,” someone has to draw a line, and no one draws that line by pure deduction.

That means legal organization, while necessary, is only one layer. It offers reference points. It does not, by itself, tell an operating company what to do on a Tuesday afternoon when a synthetic voice sounds similar to a specific person’s voice, or when an image closely resembles a known character. Who determines the degree of similarity? An automated classifier, a first-line moderator, in-house counsel, or a court? At what stage, by whom, and on what grounds is the verdict of “this is not acceptable” actually reached? Unless these questions are answered operationally, legal clarification remains suspended above the ground where decisions are made.

This is why the rights-infringement problem is also a problem of authority, responsibility, and remedy. And that is why three common vantage points, though all necessary, remain insufficient on their own.

The first is technology. Better detection models, better filtering, better data scrubbing, better output controls. These are indispensable. But technology enforces lines that have already been drawn; it cannot decide where the line should be.

The second is AI ethics. Transparency, fairness, accountability, human-centeredness. These principles matter, and they orient organizations toward legitimate values. Yet principles do not resolve individual cases. They point in a direction; they do not adjudicate a specific output.

The third is governance. Policies, committees, audit mechanisms, rules of procedure. Governance is indispensable because it creates the container in which decisions occur. But a well-governed organization can still produce incoherent judgments if the decisions inside that container are left unstructured.

Japan’s AI Business Guidelines version 1.2, published jointly by the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications and the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry, call on developers of autonomous AI agents to build mechanisms that require human judgment, in view of risks such as malfunction and privacy intrusion. The direction is right. AI should not be entrusted with everything; human judgment must remain structurally present. But “insert a human” is a requirement, not a design.

In practice, what often happens is predictable. An AI produces an initial classification. Only the ambiguous cases reach a human reviewer. The reviewer becomes fatigued, is measured on throughput, and applies criteria inconsistently. Similar cases yield different outcomes. The organization can still say a human was involved, but the quality of judgment is not preserved.

So the issue is not whether humans are present. It is whether human judgment is designed.

“Keep a human in the loop” is therefore too shallow unless the loop itself is structured. Who reviews, at what stage, with what information, against what criteria, within what time budget? Where is the decision recorded? How is it audited? How does it feed back into subsequent decisions and model behavior? Without that level of design, “a human was involved” becomes an alibi rather than an accountability structure.

At this point, the missing layer becomes visible. Rights infringement in the generative AI era is not solvable from any single layer. Technology, ethics, governance, and human involvement all matter. But something remains unaddressed unless organizations intentionally design the act of judgment itself. This layer is not DX, not governance, not automation, not AI ethics. It cuts across all of them.

Decision Design is a judgment architecture framework proposed by Ryoji Morii, founder of Insynergy Inc., for structuring authority, accountability, and decision boundaries in AI-augmented organizations.

What does that mean in practical terms? It means designing who holds decision authority, the scope of that authority, the criteria applied, the sequence of escalation, the record of judgment, and the verifiability of that record. It does not mean designing individual decisions. It means designing the structure within which decisions are produced and made institutionally legitimate.

This is where the concept of Decision Boundaries matters. A Decision Boundary is not a single line. It is a set of institutional demarcations. It answers two questions: who decides, and how far is that authority delegated before it must be taken back? An operational threshold can be tuned by an engineer for throughput. An institutional demarcation reflects who is legitimately empowered to decide. Those two things may look similar, but confusing them is how accountability quietly dissolves.

Consider a generative AI service handling voice, likeness, and character-related rights infringement. At the pre-generation prompt layer, when a prompt explicitly references a real person or a protected character, the system can perform an initial classification and refuse or warn. That can be an AI-held zone, because the criteria are relatively discrete and the cost of misclassification is relatively low. But the boundary should not be static. Refusal rates and false-refusal complaints need to be reviewed on a fixed cadence, and when thresholds are exceeded, the criteria themselves need to be revised by a human reviewer.

At the post-generation output scoring layer, outputs are measured for similarity: likeness match, voiceprint match, and other relevant metrics. The AI handles the scoring. But outputs that fall into a defined ambiguity range are routed onward. That ambiguity range is not a constant to be assumed; it is a variable to be maintained, calibrated against the service’s profile, the sensitivity of the rights-holders involved, and the historical distribution of complaints.

Then comes first-line human review. Ambiguous outputs and external complaints reach a moderator. But the moderator is not simply told to decide. They receive the flagged output, similarity scores, reference material, prior comparable decisions, a standard handling time, and clearly defined escalation criteria. Every decision is logged, not only the verdict, but the reasoning field that grounds it. This is where the record becomes structurally important.

Borderline cases then escalate to specialist and legal review. These often involve publicity rights, voice rights, deepfake edge cases, monetization decisions, and scope-of-distribution questions. Here, AI scores and similar-case references inform the process, but they do not determine the outcome. Context, intent, the nature of the harm, and the necessity of remedy are weighed together. This is explicit human assumption of authority, and what matters is that it is explicitly defined rather than left implicit.

Finally, policy-level decisions, changes to prior standards, and cases with significant social implications reach executive leadership and, where appropriate, an external committee. That is the designated point of final accountability.

What makes this system work is not merely that the layers exist. It is that they are connected. A decision at any level remains traceable through the records created at preceding stages. That is why Decision Logs matter so much. At each boundary, every decision should be captured in structured form: who decided, when, on what basis, and with reference to what material. These records serve post-hoc verification, criteria revision, response to legal inquiries, and feedback into model and process improvement.

This is different from the audit trails familiar to governance. Governance auditability asks whether the process was followed. Decision Design verifiability asks whether the judgment was sound. The first confirms procedural compliance. The second permits substantive review. In a hybrid human-AI decision chain, only structured logs preserve continuity of accountability across distributed judgment.

The practical implication for executives, CIOs, AI governance leaders, and researchers is straightforward. Boundaries must be explicit, not inherited from ad hoc practice. The AI-held zone, the first-line moderation zone, the specialist zone, the legal zone, and the executive zone should each be named, bounded, and recorded. Escalation criteria should be defined in advance and applied consistently. Log fields should capture rationale, not only outcome. Criteria should be versioned so that changes over time are traceable. Gray-zone cases should be treated as expected inputs, not as exceptions to be handled informally. Where authority is delegated to AI, the delegation must be documented as such. Where authority is taken back by humans, the reclamation must be documented as such. Where authority is suspended pending review, that suspension must be documented as such.

The coming phase of generative AI rights infringement will be less about legal interpretation in the abstract and more about operational implementation in the concrete. Law will supply reference points. Guidelines will indicate direction. Companies will build systems. What will determine the quality of those systems is not technology alone, not ethics alone, and not governance alone. It is the design of judgment itself.

As Japan’s Ministry of Justice completes its organization of the issue, the deeper organizational question remains. Who decides, where, on what basis, and with what record?
