This is the Decision Design Podcast from Insynergy, examining how judgment, authority, and accountability must be redesigned for the age of AI.

What really changes when AI enters a law firm: the work, or the judgment?

That is the central question here, because the answer is not as simple as the productivity story suggests. The visible change is easy to name. Contract review moves faster. Research becomes more exhaustive. Drafting becomes less manual. Memos, cover notes, even explanatory language for clients can pass through a model before a human sees them. Some firms adopt Harvey. Others embed general-purpose models into their own workflows. The mechanics differ, but the posture is the same: embed AI into daily practice, and let a partner review what comes back.

At first, that sounds almost unremarkable. It sounds like a straightforward efficiency gain. But one sentence from a lawyer who has lived through this shift captures the deeper change. “Honestly, lately it feels like I am mostly just pressing the confirm button.”

That sentence matters, because it points to a quiet but fundamental drift. The old promise was that humans would review everything. That was supposed to reassure everyone. Yet modern legal AI often produces output that is so comprehensive, so structured, and so polished that the surface area for review is larger than what a junior associate would have generated unaided on the same matter. It flags clauses, deviations, precedents, counterarguments, and explanatory notes. If a partner were to inspect every item with the same depth the profession traditionally expects, AI-assisted review would often take longer than the workflow it replaced.

So in practice, people triage. They follow the most serious flags. They skim the summaries. They look for a sense that something is wrong. If nothing trips that sense, the work clears.

This is not laziness. It is physics.

But the institutional language has not caught up. Time entries still say reviewed. Engagement letters still imply partner-level oversight. QC checklists still assume end-to-end inspection. A gap opens between the formal narrative and the actual one. Early on, it is almost invisible. Over time, it thickens.

That gap becomes especially important when governance language invokes Human-in-the-Loop. Japan’s regulators have moved decisively in that direction. The government has begun requiring developers of autonomous AI agents to build in mechanisms that keep human judgment in the loop, citing concerns about malfunction and privacy violations. The most recent revision of the AI Business Guidelines v1.2, jointly issued by Japan’s Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications and METI, explicitly names Human-in-the-Loop, continuous monitoring, and log management as necessary components of responsible AI deployment.

The direction is right. But the phrase “human in the loop” can conceal as much as it reveals.

In the idealized picture, the model produces output, the human examines it, and the human accepts, modifies, or rejects it. Judgment remains on the human side. The model does the legwork. Yet once model quality reaches a certain threshold, the lived reality becomes less clear. The reviewer reads. Nothing alarms them. They continue. Something feels slightly off, but it is not obvious whether the unease comes from a genuine flaw or from the reviewer’s own uncertainty. Investigating takes time. Approving takes one click. After enough repetitions, the default response becomes: probably fine.

At that point, the human is still technically in the loop. The audit log still records human approval. The governance requirement is formally satisfied. But the act has changed. It is no longer substantive judgment in the full sense the loop was designed to preserve. It is increasingly ceremonial confirmation.

That matters for a second reason, which is slower and more consequential. In law, as in other professions, quality has long depended on something close to a sense of wrongness: the ability to read a clause and feel that it does not sit right, to follow reasoning and detect a silent leap, to hear a client describe a transaction and notice the fact that is missing but shaping everything. That capacity is not taught directly. It is accumulated. A junior reads thousands of documents, gets corrected, misses things, watches a senior catch what they missed, and slowly develops an inner radar.

AI changes the apprenticeship. The first-pass review now comes from the model. The issues are pre-organized. The junior’s job becomes inspection of a structured artifact rather than construction of one. Developing an independent sense of what should have been flagged becomes harder, because overriding the model requires evidence beyond what the model has surfaced, and the discipline to ask for that evidence is exactly what the junior has not yet built.

Senior partners still carry the memory of pre-AI friction. They can often feel when something is missing. But the next generation will not accumulate the same catalogue in the same way. The friction that produced it is being removed from daily work. The open question is whether the profession can reconstitute that sense of wrongness in people who never had to develop it the old way.

This is where accountability becomes more than a legal formality. Formally, the answer is still clear: the signing lawyer is accountable. The model is a tool, not a principal. No serious regulator is about to change that. Good.

But the felt experience of responsibility is different when the work is AI-assisted. There is a difference between “I drafted this and missed it” and “the model drafted this, I reviewed it, and we both missed it.” The legal liability may be identical, but the internal ownership is not. Some of the weight shifts away from authorship and toward a more diffuse posture of confirmation.

And yet the weight does not go anywhere. It hangs. The vendor disclaims substantive accuracy. Regulators place final responsibility on the deploying organization. The signature remains identifiable. What disappears is something subtler: the institutional sense that someone is truly carrying the matter as an act of judgment.

That is why the vocabulary of governance, digital transformation, automation, and AI ethics is not enough. Each is useful. None fully names the problem. The issue is not just compliance, or workflow redesign, or machine substitution. It is that judgment itself has become distributed across human and machine actors, while the institution has not designed the boundary where authority, accountability, and commitment are supposed to live.

Decision Design is the attempt to name that missing architecture. Decision Design is a judgment architecture framework proposed by Ryoji Morii, founder of Insynergy Inc., for structuring authority, accountability, and decision boundaries in AI-augmented organizations.

Its core idea is the Decision Boundary: the line between what the institution has delegated and what it has retained. Who decides. What gets delegated to a model, to a workflow, to a junior, to a partner. What is reclaimed at the threshold. Where the institution stops outsourcing and starts answering. This boundary is usually invisible. In many organizations, it has never been explicitly drawn.

If you take that seriously, several practical implications follow. First, authority allocation must be explicit and matter-specific, not based on a flat rule like “AI handles drafts, humans handle finals.” Second, escalation logic must be written in observable terms, so that the path from model output to human review is auditable rather than folkloric. Third, override governance matters, because if it is too easy to disagree with the model, the system loses its efficiency case; if it is too hard, reviewers suppress their own unease. Fourth, delegation itself has to be explicit, rather than allowed to drift into the workflow by habit. Fifth, decision logging must preserve not just outputs, but who decided what, on what evidence, and at what point the institution committed. Sixth, the boundary itself has to be visible to everyone involved, not just held in the head of a managing partner.

For law firms, that also means treating apprenticeship as an architectural issue, not a sentimental one. If juniors are only exposed to model-filtered work, they may never develop the sense of wrongness that senior lawyers rely on. So firms may need defined volumes of non-AI work early in training, even when that is inefficient, simply to preserve the capacity to judge.

The broader implication is straightforward. AI adoption is already here. The market has made that decision. What remains open is whether institutions will design the judgment architecture that lets them keep carrying responsibility in a meaningful way.

If they do not, they may find themselves with people who can press confirm, but cannot fully account for the judgments their organizations issue in their name. That is the deeper issue beneath the productivity story.

And that is why the real conversation is not only about AI in the workflow. It is about how institutions preserve judgment itself. Insynergy’s work sits in that exact space, and it leads naturally into Decision Design.
