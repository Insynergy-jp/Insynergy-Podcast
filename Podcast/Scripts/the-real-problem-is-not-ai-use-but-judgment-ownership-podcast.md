This is the Decision Design Podcast from Insynergy, examining how judgment, authority, and accountability must be redesigned for the age of AI.

What is the real problem we should be solving in AI governance: whether AI was used, or who owns the judgment?

That question matters because we are seeing a quiet reversal in professional life. The Wall Street Journal recently reported on writers going to unusual lengths to prove they had not used AI. They leave in typos. They strip out em dashes. They rough up their prose so it sounds less polished, less symmetrical, more recognizably human. Clean, organized writing has become suspicious, so people now perform humanity for editors, clients, and algorithmic detectors that may or may not be reading.

It is, in effect, a reverse Turing test. The original asked whether a machine could pass as a human. The new one asks whether a human can prove they are not a machine. And the proof is aesthetic: messy is human, smooth is suspect. But beneath the irony lies an institutional confusion. We have begun policing the wrong variable.

“Was AI used?” has become the default question in journalism, academia, hiring, client relationships, and professional work more broadly. Yet if we think about it carefully, it is a strange question. We have always tolerated, and often celebrated, distributed production. Ghostwriters write books for executives and politicians. Editors substantially rewrite drafts while the byline remains intact. Research assistants gather sources, run preliminary analyses, and draft sections. Outsourcing firms produce slide decks, market analyses, code, and creative work for organizations that present those outputs as their own. Law firm associates write briefs that partners sign. Architecture studios depend on teams that produce the drawings the principal ultimately puts forward.

None of that has traditionally been treated as a scandal. We have understood, even if only implicitly, that professional production is distributed. The same is true in creative industries. Studio Ghibli’s films are made by hundreds of people, yet we speak of “a Miyazaki film” without hesitation. ONE PIECE is drawn with the help of a large team of assistants who handle backgrounds, screen tones, and finishing, yet it remains Eiichiro Oda’s work. Detective Conan runs through a similar production pipeline. Chibi Maruko-chan did as well. In these cases, attribution does not rest on the fantasy that one person hand-produced every frame or panel.

What sustains authorship is something else: a coherent judgment owner, someone whose decisions about what the work is, what it says, and what it commits to run consistently through what others produce. The question has never really been who held the pen. The question has been who owned the judgment.

That is the question we are now asking badly about AI. We are asking the pen question and missing the real issue.

This distinction matters because the infrastructure of authorship in modern life, including copyright, professional liability, scholarly attribution, journalistic accountability, and corporate responsibility, depends on the idea that someone owns the final shape of an output. A work becomes attributable not because every element was manually produced by one person, but because someone owns the final judgment of what the work is. That ownership is what makes it possible to say: this is what I am putting into the world; this is what I stand behind; this is what I will answer for.

That is why the model of studio authorship is so useful. When a Ghibli film is praised or criticized, Hayao Miyazaki receives the praise or the criticism, not because he personally drew every cel, but because he answered, throughout production, for what the film would be. When an Oda manga reaches a narrative turning point, the credit and scrutiny attach to him, because the judgments that shaped that moment — which panel, which beat, which expression, which line — are his, even if the ink was applied by an assistant’s hand.

We have always had this model. We have simply not had to articulate it with precision, because for most of history the alternatives were limited. Now there is a third option: AI. And it is destabilizing not because it is unprecedented, but because it forces us to make our implicit theory of authorship explicit.

The answer, as it has always been, is that authorship lives in the judgment that owns the result.

That is why Human-in-the-Loop, while sound as a slogan, is often vacant as an implementation. In practice, it frequently becomes an approval screen. The system prepares a recommendation. The human clicks approve or reject, or more often clicks approve after glancing at the summary. Done. Human present. Loop closed. Audit trail satisfied.

But human presence is not the same as human judgment. A checkbox is not a decision. A click is not deliberation. Presence in the workflow does not, by itself, mean anyone exercised meaningful authority over the outcome.

An everyday example makes this clear. Many enterprise email systems now use mis-send prevention software. They intercept outgoing messages with confirmation screens: confirm the recipients, confirm the To, Cc, and Bcc fields, confirm the attachments. At first, users pay attention. Then the confirmation becomes reflex. The brain automates any low-friction action that has produced no consequence ninety-nine times in a row. On the hundredth time, when there really is a wrong attachment or wrong recipient, the click has long since stopped being a decision.

The log still says: confirmed. Approval persists as an action. Judgment quietly disappears as a process.

This is the failure mode of Human-in-the-Loop when it is treated as a checkpoint rather than as an authority structure. The interface satisfies the requirement that a human was present. It does not satisfy the more important requirement that a human was responsible.

That problem is about to scale, because the next generation of AI systems is agentic. AI agents are increasingly drafting outbound emails, preparing proposals, summarizing legal documents, scoring candidates, pre-screening applications, routing tickets, building approval packages, generating customer responses, and even initiating outbound actions on their own. In each case, an organization can wire in a human approval step. And in each case, over time and at scale, that approval step tends toward the email-confirmation pattern: present, logged, and substantively empty.

The danger is not simply that AI is doing the work. It is that organizations may end up with the appearance of human oversight while authority over outcomes becomes diffuse, ambiguous, and ultimately unowned. The audit trail will be impeccable. Every action will have a human signature attached. And nobody, not really, will have decided anything.

A sales proposal makes this concrete. It is now technically straightforward to have AI generate an entire client proposal from a brief: executive summary, problem framing, proposed solution, pricing, timeline, risk register. The output can look credible. The format can be clean. The arguments can be coherent.

But when that proposal reaches the client meeting, a different test arrives immediately. Why this approach and not the alternative? Why is this risk acceptable and that one not? Why this priority order? What exactly are you committing to deliver?

A proposal that cannot answer these questions in the seller’s own voice is not really a proposal. It is a document shaped like one. The value of a proposal does not live in prose, layout, or visual polish. It lives in the judgments behind it: what to recommend, what to refuse, what to take on, what to put at stake.

This is why “having AI write the whole thing” tends to be hollow even when the output looks excellent. The hollowness is structural. Nobody owns the choices the document represents. But the inverse is also true. There is nothing hollow about using AI well in the same task: giving it rough notes and asking it to structure them, asking it to build a pros-and-cons table, asking it to surface missing arguments, sharpen comparison axes, refine language, and identify weak transitions. Those uses leave judgment where it belongs, with the human who can answer, in their own words, why this proposal, why this priority, why this commitment.

The boundary is not between AI and no-AI. It is between outputs whose judgments someone can defend and outputs whose judgments nobody can locate.

That distinction is now becoming institutional, not just cultural. In Japan, the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications, MIC, and the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry, METI, jointly publish the AI Business Guidelines, currently at Version 1.2. The guidelines address the risks posed by increasingly autonomous AI agents, including malfunction, privacy infringement, and unintended consequences, and call on developers and deploying organizations to build mechanisms that require human judgment in the operation of such systems.

The direction is right. Requiring that a human remain in the judgment chain is the correct instinct. But wording matters. “Requiring human judgment” can mean merely requiring a human to be present in the workflow, which leads back to the ritual problem. Or it can mean requiring that authority over the outcome be explicitly designed and assigned to a specific human role. Only the second interpretation actually solves the problem.

Policy can specify the requirement. It cannot, by itself, specify the design. That work has to happen inside organizations.

That is why governance, digital transformation, automation, and AI ethics are necessary but, taken individually, insufficient. Governance works at the level of policy, oversight, and risk classification. Digital transformation focuses on process redesign and capability building, but tends to optimize for throughput and integration rather than for who owns what decisions in the new flow. Automation focuses on efficiency and on removing tasks from human handling. AI ethics has produced important principles such as fairness, transparency, accountability, and harm avoidance, but principles are not authority structures. They tell you what to care about. They do not tell you who decides.
