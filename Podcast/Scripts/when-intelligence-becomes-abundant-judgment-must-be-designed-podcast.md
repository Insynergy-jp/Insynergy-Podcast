This is the Decision Design Podcast from Insynergy, examining how judgment, authority, and accountability must be redesigned for the age of AI.

What happens when intelligence becomes abundant, but judgment does not?

Craig Mundie’s argument, as reported in the Business Insider interview, is a useful starting point because it does not simply ask whether education should adapt to AI. It asks something more basic: what kind of education remains meaningful when AI can deliver personalized instruction, organize research, and generate answers at a scale no classroom can match? Mundie, former Chief Research and Strategy Officer at Microsoft from 2006 to 2012 and co-author of Genesis with Eric Schmidt and Henry Kissinger, argues for the integration of STEM and the liberal arts, for AI tutors, and for a reexamination of the classroom model itself. That argument is clear, and in many respects correct.

But it still leaves the deeper structural issue untouched. What AI changes is not only curriculum. It changes the relationship between knowledge and judgment.

For a long time, education reform has treated the central question as, what should students learn? That question seems practical. It assumes that the future can be anticipated well enough to define the right mix of skills, methods, and content in advance. So the system is updated: coding is added, inquiry-based learning is emphasized, active learning is promoted, STEAM frameworks are introduced. Yet all of these reforms preserve the same assumption underneath them, namely that education is mainly about pre-loading correct answers.

AI makes that assumption less and less defensible. It does not necessarily change which answers are correct. What it changes is the speed and density with which answers are produced. A large language model can return a structured response in seconds. Research, comparison, synthesis, drafting—tasks that once required sustained human effort are compressed into moments. The result is not simply that humans get more information. It is that they are confronted with answers before they have finished thinking.

That changes the burden on the human side of the process. The critical question becomes not, what can AI answer? but, what must humans retain? And once AI begins to absorb portions of intellectual work, that question appears everywhere. In education, in organizations, in policy, in professional practice. The problem is no longer whether to delegate to AI. It is where the human boundary must remain.

This is where cognitive compression becomes important. On the surface, AI appears to reduce the burden on decision-makers. It gathers material, structures options, and produces recommendations quickly. But the practical effect is often the opposite. As the speed of input preparation rises, the frequency of judgment rises with it. Each AI-generated option requires assessment. Is it adequate? Is it appropriate? Should it be accepted, modified, or rejected? And unlike older work environments, there is often no natural pause. AI operates at machine speed; the human is expected to keep up.

That is a different problem from information overload. Information overload concerns attention. Cognitive compression concerns decision. The bottleneck moves from perception to judgment.

Mundie’s call to integrate STEM with the humanities becomes more significant in that light. Technical understanding alone is not enough, and neither is humanistic reflection alone. But even combined, they do not automatically produce the capacity for judgment. Judgment has to be trained deliberately. It has to be treated not as a vague virtue, but as a competency that can be designed into institutions.

That leads to a different framing of the question. If AI tutors can handle personalized and adaptive instruction, then knowledge transmission can be delegated. The classroom changes. The teacher changes. The student changes. But then we must ask: what does the human teacher retain ownership of? What does the student retain ownership of? And who decides where the line is drawn between what is delegated and what is retained?

That line is not a minor operational detail. It is the core issue. Between delegation and ownership, there is always a boundary. Where that boundary sits is not just a technical question, and it is not just a question of individual judgment. It is a design question. If institutions do not define it, the boundary will still exist, but it will be implicit, unstable, and often invisible.

In education, that creates obvious but unresolved problems. When AI supports a student’s learning process, who performs the final verification of understanding? When a student relies on an AI-generated answer, where does accountability for that judgment sit? When a teacher uses AI-generated analysis to assign a grade, who holds evaluative authority? These questions are not theoretical. Institutions can deploy AI without answering them, but they cannot control the consequences afterward.

This is the point at which Decision Design matters. Decision Design is a judgment architecture framework proposed by Ryoji Morii, founder of Insynergy Inc., for structuring authority, accountability, and decision boundaries in AI-augmented organizations.

The point is to treat judgment not as a private cognitive act alone, but as a structural phenomenon that can be deliberately designed. In any decision process, someone decides, on some basis, within some scope. AI changes all three. It gathers information, generates options, and often produces recommendations. Under those conditions, the distribution of judgment between human and machine can no longer remain tacit. Decision Design makes that distribution explicit.

It is important to be precise about what that does and does not mean. Decision Design is not strategy, because strategy concerns what to pursue, while Decision Design concerns who decides and how far that authority extends. It is not ethics, because ethics asks what is right, while Decision Design asks who bears responsibility for determining what is right. It is not digital transformation, because digital transformation concerns what to automate, while Decision Design concerns what remains after automation and how that residual judgment is structured.

At the center of this approach is the concept of Decision Boundary, the explicit demarcation of where AI authority ends and human authority begins within a given decision process. That matters because in AI-augmented workflows, the surface structure can be misleading. AI proposes, human approves. But if the human approves without substantive evaluation, then the human is only formally the decision-maker. Functionally, the judgment has already migrated to the machine.

The boundary makes that visible. It identifies where substantive judgment actually resides, not merely where authority appears to reside. It also clarifies what “letting AI handle it” really means. Delegating information gathering is not the same as delegating option filtering. Delegating option filtering is not the same as delegating recommendation generation. And recommendation generation is not the same as executing a recommendation without review. At each stage, human judgment is altered in a different way. The boundary needs to be defined stage by stage.

That is also where accountability becomes concrete. When judgment is ambiguous, accountability becomes ambiguous. If AI proposes and a human approves, and the outcome is harmful, current frameworks often struggle to say clearly who is responsible. The proposer? The approver? The system designer? Decision Design addresses that by predefining judgment authority at each stage. Responsibility is then attributable by design, not reconstructed after failure.

Applied to education, this yields practical institutional changes. Students can submit decision logs that show not only the final work product, but the judgment process behind it: what they asked the AI, what they accepted, what they rejected, and why. That does not prohibit AI use. It makes the judgment layer visible. Judgment ownership mapping can require students, before a project begins, to specify who or what has authority at each phase. Boundary configuration exercises can give identical assignments with different AI-human boundaries, so students experience how the placement of the boundary changes the result. And at advanced levels, AI co-decision curricula can train students to document why a particular allocation of judgment was made in a real decision sequence.

The practical implication is straightforward. Organizations and educational institutions should stop treating AI adoption as only a matter of efficiency, capability, or content delivery. They need to define the decision boundary first, then assign accountability, then build workflows and curricula around that structure. Otherwise, they will automate the front end of judgment while leaving the back end undefined.

Mundie is right that education must change. But the deeper shift is from education that transmits knowledge to education that designs judgment. That shift is not limited to schools. It applies wherever AI enters intellectual work, which means healthcare, finance, law, public administration, and beyond. Education matters first because it is where future decision-makers are formed.

And that is why the broader question is not simply how to teach in the age of AI, but how to structure judgment in the age of abundant intelligence. That is the work Insynergy is taking forward through Decision Design.
