This is the Decision Design Podcast from Insynergy, examining how judgment, authority, and accountability must be redesigned for the age of AI.

What is the quiet, structural damage when AI takes over the work that used to make people capable?

A familiar labor story keeps appearing. A government releases a projection. The numbers suggest that, by some future year, office workers will be in surplus and AI-related specialists will be in shortage. Commentators respond in the usual pattern: some warn of an AI job apocalypse, others say retraining will solve it, and a few question the projections. Then the conversation moves on.

Recently, Japan’s Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry released one such projection. It said that by 2040, the country would face roughly 4.4 million surplus clerical workers, a shortage of about 3.4 million AI- and robotics-capable specialists, and another 2.6 million missing in frontline trades. The headlines wrote themselves: clerical workers in surplus, AI specialists in shortage, retrain or perish.

But that framing is misleading, not because the numbers are necessarily wrong, but because they describe a symptom while the conversation keeps mistaking it for the disease.

What is actually happening is quieter and harder to see. The work AI is absorbing is not just work. In many organizations, it was also the substrate through which inexperienced people gradually became experienced ones. When that substrate disappears, we do not simply lose jobs. We lose the conditions under which the next generation of judgment-capable humans was supposed to form.

That is the part no projection captures.

The shallow story is easy to tell: AI automates tasks, the tasks cluster in office work, some office workers are displaced, retraining moves them into roles AI cannot yet do, the economy adjusts. Each step contains a small lie of compression.

Start with the idea that AI is automating tasks. It is more accurate to say that AI is automating a layer of work where inputs are structured, outputs are predictable, and the room for judgment is narrow. That layer cuts across job titles. It exists inside lawyering, engineering, design, consulting, and journalism. So when we talk about clerical work being automated, we are using a 1950s job category to describe a 2026 phenomenon. The mismatch matters.

Then comes the assumption that workers can simply be moved. The implicit model is that a human in role A can be retrained into role B, like software recompiling for a new platform. That works for narrow skills. It does not work for the kind of capability that takes years of being slowly wrong before you become reliably right.

And then there is the deepest compression of all, in the word adjusts. The economy adjusts, as if the only thing being rebalanced were labor units, rather than the machinery by which a society produces capable adults.

What entry-level work was actually for becomes clear if you have managed a junior employee. In the first year or two, their work is rarely for the work itself.

A junior analyst takes meeting notes. The notes are useful, but the real point is that taking them forces the analyst to follow the meeting closely enough to understand who decides, who is performing certainty, who is hedging, and what the unspoken disagreements are about. The notes are the tuition; the meeting is the school.

A junior associate drafts a client deck. It is rough, and the senior will rewrite half of it. But making it requires the junior to choose what to lead with, where to place numbers, and how to phrase the recommendation. The senior’s rewrite is the lesson. Without the first draft, there is nothing to rewrite.

A junior engineer fixes the tiny bug nobody wants to look at. In the process, they read code written by other people, in styles they did not choose, solving problems they did not pose. The bug fix is the deliverable; the orientation through someone else’s reasoning is the actual asset.

Every white-collar profession has some version of this. The visible output is one thing. The hidden curriculum is another. For most of the modern era, they were entangled by accident. The work happened to require junior labor, and the junior labor happened to produce learning as a byproduct.

Now that entanglement is disappearing without anyone deciding to remove it. AI does the meeting notes. AI does the first draft of the deck. AI fixes the tiny bug. The output gets produced faster and often better. The byproduct, the slow and granular education of a human being, simply stops happening.

Nobody is opposed to this in the abstract. The transition is happening because each individual decision to use AI is locally sensible. It is the aggregate that should worry us.

Most discussion of AI and work is conducted in the vocabulary of skills. People need new skills. Retraining is about skills. The shortage is a skills gap. But that vocabulary quietly conflates two very different forms of human capability.

Skills are things you can be taught. Programming languages, statistical methods, financial modeling, project management frameworks, written persuasion in a particular genre. Skills travel. They can be acquired in months, sometimes weeks.

Judgment is different.

Judgment is what remains after thousands of small decisions under incomplete information, some of them went badly, and you sat with the consequences long enough to adjust and try again. It is not portable in the way a skill is portable. It cannot be transferred from one person’s head to another’s. It cannot be reliably manufactured by a curriculum, because the essential ingredient is responsibility for an outcome you only partly controlled.

Judgment compounds slowly. It accumulates almost invisibly over years and then, one day, manifests as a person who can walk into a meeting, scan the room, ask three questions, and identify the actual problem. The walking-in takes seconds. The accumulating took a decade of being given just enough rope to occasionally make small mistakes.

Senior people are valuable not because they possess facts a junior could be tested on, but because they have run thousands of small cycles of decide, observe, revise, and the residue of those cycles is what we call experience.

It helps to treat experience as a form of capital. Experience capital accrues only through time spent doing the thing. There is no synthetic substitute. You cannot front-load it. You cannot inject it. You cannot buy it and transfer it into your own balance sheet. It depreciates. People retire. People burn out. The stock constantly leaks and has to be replenished by people coming up through the layers and accumulating their own. And it requires a specific environment to form at all: real decisions, real stakes, and someone more senior watching closely enough to tell you what you missed without rescuing you from having seen it.

For a long time, entry-level cognitive work accidentally provided that environment. The work was real. The stakes were real, even if modest. There was usually someone more senior whose job partially included noticing what the junior was getting wrong. The environment was not designed to produce judgment. It just did, as a byproduct of how organizations had to be staffed.

That environment is now being dismantled in the name of efficiency, faster than most people seem to notice.

The picture is not one-sided. For people who have already accumulated significant experience capital, AI can feel like a miracle. A senior consultant can use it to produce in a week what used to take a team a month. A senior engineer can ship features that once required more coordination. A senior researcher can read across literatures they never would have had time to read.

AI is, for those people, a kind of cognitive prosthesis. It absorbs the rote layer of their work and amplifies the judgment layer, which they already have in abundance.

That is the source of much of the genuine optimism in current AI discourse, and it is not wrong. The one-person company that does what used to require ten people is not hype; it is already happening in narrow domains and will keep happening. The discourse about solopreneurs running fully leveraged operations with AI agents handling coding, sales, support, and operations is real. Sam Altman has talked publicly about the eventual emergence of the first one-person billion-dollar company. Whether or not that specific milestone arrives on schedule, the underlying trend is visible across several industries.

But the people running these leveraged operations are, almost without exception, people who put in their time before the tools existed. They became senior in an environment that still had the apprenticeship layer. They have the judgment because they once had the boring work. They acquired pattern recognition because they once spent years in rooms where patterns were forming, taking notes nobody read.

AI amplifies them because they were already amplifiable. It does not manufacture the underlying judgment. It augments what is already there.

You can see the asymmetry everywhere. A senior software architect may now ship more code than ever, but that architect is also relying on years of pattern recognition to know when an AI-generated solution is structurally wrong. A senior investigative editor reviewing AI-drafted copy is checking it against an internal model of how a story should land, built over decades. The model is doing the work. The model took thirty years to build. The AI did not help build it.

This is the crucial asymmetry. The same technology that turns an experienced operator into a one-person company is simultaneously eliminating the entry-level work that would have produced the next generation of experienced operators. Amplification and erosion are happening at the same time, driven by the same tools.

Now, in hiring conversations, a related pattern appears. Companies say they cannot find good people, meaning people who are already capable. The job postings ask for five years of experience, demonstrated track record, prior ownership of similar problems. The implicit message is: we want someone already finished. We do not want to do the finishing.

Job seekers, for their part, want companies that will invest in them. They want training, mentorship, growth, a path. The implicit message is: we want someone else to do the finishing for us.
