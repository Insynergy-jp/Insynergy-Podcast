This is the Decision Design Podcast from Insynergy, examining how judgment, authority, and accountability must be redesigned for the age of AI.

The central question is this: is “human-in-the-loop” enough to govern autonomous AI, or do organizations need something more precise, namely a way to design judgment itself?

Requiring humans to stay in the loop is the right instinct. It is not, by itself, a governance design. That distinction matters more now because, in March 2026, Japan’s Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications and Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry jointly published a draft revision of the country’s AI Business Guidelines. As reported by Nikkei CrossTech on March 16, the revision marks a meaningful shift in how governments are beginning to think about autonomous AI systems.

The core move is straightforward but significant. The Japanese government is moving to require developers and deploying firms to build mechanisms that make human judgment mandatory for autonomous AI agents, in light of malfunction and privacy risks. The draft introduces formal definitions for AI agents and physical AI systems, adopts a risk-based approach to governance, and recommends a principle of minimum necessary authority for AI agents, limiting what autonomous systems are permitted to act upon without human involvement.

It also addresses specific failure modes: unintended transaction execution, deletion of critical data, physical malfunctions, and risks to professional and educational domains where AI substitution could erode human judgment at scale.

This is not merely a compliance update. It is a signal that the era of deploying AI agents without institutionally defined boundaries is ending, at least at the level of regulatory expectation. But the deeper question remains whether the concept at the center of this shift, human judgment in the loop, is sufficient to meet the governance challenge it is trying to address.

It is not. And the reason is structural.

Human-in-the-Loop is a sound principle. It says that autonomous AI systems should include human checkpoints, moments where a person reviews, approves, or overrides AI-generated outputs or actions. In high-stakes domains, that is plainly better than full AI autonomy without human recourse. No serious organization should treat an AI system as governable if no human can intervene.

Yet the limitation appears as soon as one tries to implement the principle inside an actual organization. Consider a credit analyst signing off on a risk score generated by an AI model. Consider a legal reviewer reading a contract draft produced by an AI system. In both cases, a human is in the loop. In both cases, the formal condition would appear to be satisfied.

But the governance questions are different, and they are the ones that matter. Does the analyst have authority to override the model? Is there a defined condition under which the score must be escalated? Is the basis for the sign-off recorded anywhere? In the contract example, what does review actually mean: format checking, substantive legal analysis, or final authorization? And who bears accountability if the contract contains an error?

Human-in-the-Loop does not answer these questions because it is concerned with presence, not structure. It tells us whether a human is there. It does not tell us what that human is authorized to decide, on what basis, within what constraints, and with what accountability trail.

That gap matters because the real governance problem introduced by AI agents is not primarily one of oversight. It is one of authority.

In traditional organizations, authority over decisions is relatively legible. Approval chains exist. Escalation paths are defined. Accountability is tied to roles and documented through process. These structures are never perfect, but they are structures. When AI agents enter the picture, the distribution of authority becomes ambiguous in a more difficult way. The AI performs analysis, generates recommendations, or, in agentic configurations, executes actions directly. A human is present somewhere in the process. Yet the division between AI judgment and human judgment is often left implicit.

The result is accountability diffusion. The human has technically approved something, but without a defined scope of review, without a clear escalation condition, and without a record of the reasoning behind the approval. The form of oversight is present. The substance of judgment is not.

This is where Decision Design becomes necessary. Decision Design is not a tool for improving individual decisions. It is a discipline for designing the authority structure within which decisions are made, transferred, and made accountable across human-AI systems.

Decision Design is the practice of explicitly defining which decisions belong to AI systems, which belong to human agents, how authority transfers between them, and how the exercise of that authority is recorded. It treats judgment not as a natural human act that merely needs to be preserved, but as a structural element of organizational governance that must be deliberately designed.

It is important to be clear about what it is not. It is not a decision-support tool, not a dashboard, not an alert system, not a general set of ethical principles for AI behavior, and not the same thing as Human-in-the-Loop implementation. All of those operate within a decision structure. Decision Design is concerned with the structure itself.

“Decision Design is a judgment architecture framework proposed by Ryoji Morii, founder of Insynergy Inc., for structuring authority, accountability, and decision boundaries in AI-augmented organizations.”

The operational concept at the center of Decision Design is the Decision Boundary. A Decision Boundary is the explicit demarcation of where AI judgment ends and human judgment begins, or more precisely, where one form of legitimate authority ends and another begins.

These boundaries are not static thresholds or performance cutoffs. They are defined in terms of risk type, consequence reversibility, domain-specific accountability requirements, and organizational authority structures. They are institutional demarcations of legitimate authority.

That distinction is crucial. Operational thresholds are typically set by technical teams and adjusted as model performance improves. Institutional demarcations, by contrast, are set by governance structures and reflect organizational accountability, legal exposure, and the meaning of a decision in context.

In practical terms, designing Decision Boundaries requires decomposing what is often called human review into distinct acts. There is formal review: does the output conform to required specifications? There is substantive review: is the content appropriate given the full context? And there is final authorization: does the accountable authority affirm the decision and accept responsibility for its consequences? These are different judgment acts. They require different expertise, different authority, and they carry different accountability implications. Conflating them under “human approval” is where governance collapses into compliance theater.

Decision Boundaries also need explicit escalation conditions, the circumstances under which an AI system must halt autonomous action and route to human judgment, and explicit stopping conditions for agentic systems operating in high-stakes environments. The Japanese government’s draft guidelines implicitly call for both. Decision Design provides the structural vocabulary to specify them.

But defining authority structures is not enough. A governance architecture that does not record the exercise of authority is incomplete. That is the function of Decision Logs.

A Decision Log is not an output record. It is not a system audit trail that records what an AI produced. A Decision Log records the exercise of judgment: who held authority over a given decision, what basis they used, how their judgment related to the AI’s output, and what accountability they accepted in making the decision.

Decision Logs preserve accountability continuity across distributed judgment processes. In AI-augmented workflows, accountability is spread across multiple actors and multiple moments: the AI generates, a reviewer assesses, an authorizing agent approves, and an executing system acts. Without a Decision Log that captures the judgment exercise at each stage, the accountability chain cannot be reconstructed after the fact. That matters for internal governance, regulatory scrutiny, legal exposure, and organizational learning.

The difference between a sign-off and a Decision Log entry is simple but decisive. A sign-off records that a human was present. A Decision Log records what that human decided, on what basis, and within what defined scope of authority. The first satisfies the form of Human-in-the-Loop. The second is what makes governance real.

For organizations implementing AI agents in consequential workflows, the practical implications are direct. First, map judgment points across the workflow and make every decision point explicit. Second, classify decisions by authority type, distinguishing what can be delegated to AI, what requires human substantive judgment, and what requires formal human authorization. Third, decompose review into formal review, substantive review, and final authorization, and define who may perform each. Fourth, define escalation and stopping conditions for autonomous action. Fifth, design Decision Logs that capture the exercise of judgment, not just the output of AI processes. Sixth, revisit those boundaries over time, because Decision Boundaries are not permanent; they must evolve as capability, context, and regulation change.

This is the practical synthesis. The Japanese revision points in the right direction by requiring human judgment to be built into the governance of autonomous AI systems. But organizations should not stop at human presence. They need structurally defined authority, clear boundaries, explicit escalation, and durable records of judgment. That is what allows accountability to remain legible when AI becomes operationally embedded.

The next phase of AI governance is not primarily about oversight. It is about the design of judgment architecture, making explicit what has been left implicit, and making accountable what has been left diffuse. That is the work Insynergy is advancing through Decision Design.
